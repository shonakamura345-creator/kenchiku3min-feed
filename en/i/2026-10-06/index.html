Hiroshima plans a 10,000-capacity arena by its station, to be built privately and given to the city

A plan has been presented for a new arena for about 10,000 people on the former JR West Hiroshima branch office site near the Shinkansen exit of Hiroshima Station. It targets 232 days of use and 1.6 million visitors a year, with opening planned for fiscal 2031.

A plan for a new arena with a capacity of about 10,000 has taken shape on the former site of West Japan Railway Company's (JR West) Hiroshima branch office, in the Futabanosato area of Higashi Ward in Hiroshima, western Japan. At a public-private council meeting on Oct 1, JR West, which owns the land, presented a draft business plan. According to Hiroshima Television, it would be a multipurpose arena used mainly for basketball, also hosting live concerts, MICE events (meetings, incentive travel, conferences and exhibitions) and ceremonies. It aims for 232 days of use and 1.6 million visitors a year, with opening targeted for fiscal 2031 (Japan's fiscal year starts in April).

According to the Nikkei, the council confirmed that it will mainly study a “conditional donation” approach, in which the private sector builds the facility and donates it to the government on the condition that it runs the venue. The project would be led mainly by JR West and the Hiroshima Dragonflies, a professional basketball team, and the cost has been reported as possibly exceeding ¥30 billion.

Hiroshima Television also reported that a plan was presented to connect Hiroshima Station and the arena directly at the second-floor level. The site is a few minutes' walk from the station's Shinkansen (bullet train) exit, and if fans can get from the station to their seats without going outdoors, it would be a station-integrated venue rare among arenas in Japan. At the site, construction fencing has gone up around the old branch office building as preparations for demolition move ahead.

Briefing
  • The council includes Hiroshima Prefecture, the City of Hiroshima, JR West and the Hiroshima Dragonflies, among others. Planned uses are basketball, other sports, live concerts, MICE, ceremonies and events (Hiroshima Television, Saitoshika)
  • The candidate site is in Futabanosato 3-chome, Higashi Ward, Hiroshima, a few minutes' walk from the Shinkansen exit of JR Hiroshima Station. As of September 2026, construction fencing had gone up around the former JR West Hiroshima branch office building (AB Hiroshima)
  • In Hiroshima Prefecture, the Dragonflies' home is Hiroshima Sun Plaza, which holds about 5,000, and an arena for 10,000 would be the first in the prefecture. According to the Nikkei, the public-private council has been studying the idea since summer 2026

The city owns it, the private sector runs it. Thirty years from now, who will end up paying to repair the building?

Background
What a conditional donation is

In this approach, a private party builds a public facility with its own money and donates it to the local government with certain conditions attached, such as running it. The local government gets the facility without paying for construction, and the private side gets long-term operating rights. Similar public-private splits have been used for a new arena in Aichi Prefecture and for ballparks around Japan, and the arrangement requires a vote by the local assembly.

Japan's arena building boom

Since the B.League, Japan's professional basketball league, made 5,000-plus seats and new arenas part of its criteria for the top division from 2026, plans for arenas of around 10,000 seats have been popping up across the country. Kobe's GLION ARENA, Nagasaki and Saga have led the way, and the dominant model combines sports and music to fill as many days of use as possible.

Japan's antitrust watchdog finds 42 firms rigged condo repair bids; ministry sets up review panel

Japan's Fair Trade Commission found that two design consultants and 40 contractors rigged 171 large-scale condominium repair projects in the Kanto region around Tokyo. The construction ministry has set up a panel on fairer ordering, which first meets on Oct 19.

On Sept 28, the Japan Fair Trade Commission (JFTC) found that 42 companies, two design consultants and 40 contractors, restricted competition by coordinating who would win 171 large-scale repair projects. The work was ordered through design consultants by condominium owners' management associations in Tokyo and the six surrounding prefectures of the Kanto region. The JFTC issued cease-and-desist orders to 38 companies and ordered 37 contractors to pay surcharges totaling ¥1,637.88 million. It is the first time the JFTC has found a violation of the Antimonopoly Act and penalized companies over condominium repair work.

In response, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) announced on Sept 29 that it will set up a study group on making the ordering of large-scale condominium repair work fairer. According to NHK, the group will be chaired by Shuichi Matsumura, president of Kobe Design University, and will hold its first meeting on Oct 19. It will review the current system, in which management associations choose contractors through design consultants, and consider ways to make it fairer.

Large-scale repairs are carried out every 12 to 15 years, fixing exterior walls, roof waterproofing, and water supply and drain pipes all at once, and it is not unusual for them to cost hundreds of millions of yen per building. The cost is paid from repair reserve funds that residents have built up, so any bid-rigging hits residents' wallets directly.

Briefing
  • Cease-and-desist orders went to 38 companies, and surcharge payment orders to 37 contractors. The two design consultants, Rinoshisu Corporation and Sho Sekkei, were both among those given cease-and-desist orders (JFTC)
  • The study group's first meeting runs from 3 to 5 p.m. on Oct 19 at the Central Government Building No. 2 in Tokyo, with online participation also available. The chair is Shuichi Matsumura, president of Kobe Design University (MLIT, NHK)
  • MLIT published surveys of large-scale condominium repair work in 2021 and 2024, showing the distribution of construction costs per unit as a benchmark for management associations judging whether estimates are reasonable

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

A large-scale condo repair is the job that comes around once every 12 to 15 years, when scaffolding goes up over the whole building and the exterior walls and roof get fixed. For the people living there, it's something “the management association decided,” so it's hard to see what's going on inside, right? Here's how this case worked. Management associations are amateurs when it comes to construction, so they hire a design consultant to write the specifications and pick a contractor. Those consultants teamed up with the contractors and decided in advance who would win. In other words, the party that was supposed to be on the residents' side was on the other side. As an architect, this is the part I take most seriously. Design and supervision are valuable precisely because they are independent from the people doing the construction. It's like a hospital: you don't want the doctor making the diagnosis and the company selling the drugs to be one and the same. At MLIT's study group, the issues should be how to guarantee consultants' neutrality and how to make comparing estimates more transparent. The surcharges total ¥1,637.88 million. That money started out as the repair reserves residents have paid in every month.

Background
Two ways to order repairs

Large-scale repairs are ordered in one of two main ways. In the design-supervision method, a design consultant writes the specifications and has contractors compete; in the contractor-led method, one contractor handles everything from the survey to the construction. The design-supervision method has the advantage of bringing in a third party, but this case exposed its weak point: if the consultant stops being neutral, competition stops working.

Underfunded repair reserves

According to an MLIT survey, more than 30% of condominiums have repair reserves that fall short of their plans. With material and labor costs rising and pushing up construction costs, overpriced contracts drain reserves faster, leading to postponed repairs and deteriorating buildings. Fair ordering is also about protecting residents' assets.

Nara seeks private partner to run 9-hectare park at the Heijo Palace site for 30 years

Nara Prefecture has opened a call for an operator to develop and run the roughly 9-hectare (22 acres) prefectural section of the Heijo Palace Site Historical Park. Privately built and run dining and lodging facilities and a pedestrian bridge are planned, with opening targeted for April 2031.

Nara Prefecture published a call for proposals on Oct 1 for a project to develop and operate the prefectural park area of the Heijo Palace Site Historical Park. According to the Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, the project covers about 3.1 hectares (7.7 acres) west of Suzaku Avenue, about 0.7 hectares (1.7 acres) on the east side, and about 5 hectares (12 acres) south of the palace site, over a project period of about 32 years. The reference price for the service fees the prefecture will pay over that period is about ¥8.5 billion including tax.

In the east and south areas, new amenity facilities will be built and run by the private sector based on the operator's proposals. According to Saitoshika, these could include restaurants, shops, lodging and hands-on experience facilities, with a building footprint of up to about 12,500 m² (135,000 sq ft). The project uses a build-own-operate (BOO) private finance initiative (PFI), in which the private side builds, owns and runs the facilities, while the prefecture pays for public facilities such as paths, plazas and a pedestrian bridge. The operating period is 30 years, with construction expected to start in April 2029 and the park to open in April 2031.

The Heijo Palace site was the heart of Heijo-kyo, Japan's capital in the 8th century, in what is now the city of Nara. It is a Special Historic Site, the highest grade of historic site designation in Japan, and the national government park there has reconstructed buildings such as the Daigokuden (Imperial Audience Hall) and the Suzakumon gate. The prefectural area is the gateway spreading out to the south, linking the historic site and the city across the Kintetsu Nara Line, a private railway.

Briefing
  • The prefectural park area totals about 9 hectares (22 acres): west of Suzaku Avenue (about 3.1 ha), the east side (about 0.7 ha, excluding a city road) and south of the palace site (about 5 ha). Privately built and run facilities will go in the east and south areas (Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun)
  • The operator is expected to set up a special purpose company (SPC) to run the project, with construction starting in April 2029, opening in April 2031 and a 30-year operating period. The prefecture will recruit and select the operator during fiscal 2026 (Nara Prefecture, Saitoshika)
  • The national Heijo Palace Site Historical Park displays the First Daigokuden (reconstructed in 2010), the Suzakumon gate (reconstructed in 1998) and a replica of a ship that carried Japan's envoys to Tang-dynasty China. The prefectural area sits midway between Kintetsu's Yamato-Saidaiji and Shin-Omiya stations, south of the historic site across the tracks

If you've ever walked around the Heijo Palace site, I bet you were surprised by how big it is. It's a grassy field 1.3 km (0.8 miles) east to west and 1 km (0.6 miles) north to south, with the reconstructed Suzakumon gate and Daigokuden hall standing on it. The historic site itself is a national government park, and you can hardly build anything there. The prefectural area this time is on its southern entrance side. Here, private companies will build restaurants and lodging with their own money and run them for 30 years. The prefecture pays only for the paths, plazas and pedestrian bridge. Hearing “lodging next to a historic site” might make you tense up, but I actually see it as a design challenge that could only exist here. How tall, and under what kind of roof, should a building face Suzaku Avenue, the axis of a capital from 1,300 years ago? The pedestrian bridge is there because, in reality, train tracks and roads cut across the historic site. The call for proposals has only just started, and the operators' proposals are still to come. How will designers draw the buildings that stand in front of the Suzakumon gate? I'll be watching patiently.

Background
A national and prefectural split

The Heijo Palace Site Historical Park has two layers: the core of the historic site is developed by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism as a national government park, while Nara Prefecture develops the gateway areas around it as a prefectural park. Heritage protection rules are strict in the national area, so visitor functions such as dining and lodging are handled by the prefectural area.

The spread of Park-PFI

Since a 2017 amendment to Japan's Urban Parks Act created a public tender system for installing and managing park facilities, known as Park-PFI, the approach of putting private revenue-generating facilities in parks to cover development and maintenance costs has spread nationwide. In parks next to historic sites, limits on scenery and building height are strict, so balancing profitability with heritage becomes the key question in choosing an operator.
