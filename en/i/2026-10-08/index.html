Japan sets April 1, 2027 for written contracts on all architectural design work, whatever the size

Japan's construction ministry announced on Oct 6 that the cabinet approved an ordinance putting the contract provisions of the revised Kenchikushi Act into force on April 1, 2027. From then on, every design and construction supervision contract must be in writing, regardless of floor area.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) announced on Oct 6 that the cabinet had approved the government ordinances needed to bring into force the Act Partially Amending the Kenchikushi Act (Act No. 74 of 2026). The Kenchikushi Act is the law that licenses architects in Japan and regulates their offices. According to BuildApp News, the cabinet approved two ordinances. One puts the revised law's provisions on contracts into force on April 1, 2027, and the other makes operational updates to the Act's enforcement order. The provisions taking effect require architect offices to put every contract for design and construction supervision (the architect's job of checking that construction matches the drawings) in writing, and add a best-effort obligation to prepare estimates that show how fees are calculated.

Under the current system, the mutual exchange of written contracts is mandatory only for projects such as new buildings with a total floor area of more than 300 m² (3,200 sq ft). Below that, written contracts have merely been described as desirable. The revised law passed the House of Councillors, the upper house of Japan's parliament, on July 24 and was promulgated on July 31. It was structured so that the parts on contracts and estimates take effect on a date set by ordinance within one year of promulgation, and the parts on license requirements, such as allowing students to take the kenchikushi exam while still in school, within three years. This ordinance sets the date for the former.

The ordinances do not cover the start dates for the licensing changes, such as relaxed exam eligibility and shorter practical experience requirements for second-class and wooden-building (mokuzo) kenchikushi, which are expected to be set separately. With about half a year until the rules take effect, offices that design small houses and renovations are entering a period of reviewing how they handle contracts and estimates.

  • The contract provisions take effect on April 1, 2027. Every design and construction supervision contract will have to be in writing
  • Preparing estimates is a best-effort obligation. Start dates for the licensing changes, such as exam eligibility, are expected to be set by a separate ordinance

Making written contracts mandatory across the board touches the very way we run architect offices. For houses and shops of 300 m² (3,200 sq ft) or less, it's still not unusual for a design job to start with nothing more than a conversation and some drawings. The design fee and the scope of work stay vague, and it all ends in a dispute. This revision is the law stepping in to stop that. Preparing an estimate is only a best-effort obligation, but I don't think it's a small thing that the idea of showing how a fee is calculated is now written into the law. Even for small shop interior renovations, I attach an itemized breakdown with the reasoning behind each item to my rough estimates. When you write down the reasoning, your own scope of work naturally gets sorted out too. At your office, how much effort does it take to get a design contract down in writing? The rules take effect on April 1, 2027. You have half a year to get your contract and estimate templates in order.

Background
Written contracts under current rules

The revised Kenchikushi Act that took effect in 2015 required the parties to design and construction supervision contracts for projects such as new buildings with a total floor area of more than 300 m² (3,200 sq ft) to exchange written documents setting out the required terms. For 300 m² (3,200 sq ft) or less there was no legal obligation, and written contracts were only encouraged.

A revision in two stages

The 2026 revision of the Kenchikushi Act takes effect in two parts, each on a date set by ordinance. The parts on contracts and estimates come within one year of promulgation, and the parts on license requirements, such as opening the exam to students still in school, within three years. The law was promulgated on July 31, 2026.

Tokyo's Nakano Ward delays the Nakano Sun Plaza rebuilding plan by three months to May 2027

Nakano Ward has pushed back the revision of its redevelopment plan for Nakano Sun Plaza, whose earlier scheme was scrapped, from February to May 2027. The ward said it needs more time after hearing from an expert panel and others.

Tokyo's Nakano Ward announced on Oct 5 that it will delay by three months, from February to May 2027, the revision of its redevelopment project plan for Nakano Sun Plaza, the landmark concert hall complex next to Nakano Station. The plan sets the direction for the site's redevelopment, after the earlier scheme was scrapped because of soaring project costs. According to the Asahi Shimbun, the ward decided it needed more time to deliberate after hearing from an expert panel that first met on Sep 18 and from the ward assembly. The ward gave the same explanation at the ward assembly's construction committee on Oct 6.

In August the ward put together an outline for the revision. It set about 1,100 homes as a rough upper limit for housing in the new complex, took a hall for about 3,000 people as the baseline, and proposed using a fixed-term land lease on part of the site. Under the original timeline, a draft was due in October and the revision in February 2027.

The ward is aiming to start construction in fiscal 2030 and finish in fiscal 2034 (Japan's fiscal year runs from April to March). According to the Asahi Shimbun, a senior ward official said, "It's a delay of a few months, so it shouldn't have much effect on the construction schedule itself." The Tokyo Shimbun, however, reported that the ward has not said clearly whether the fiscal 2034 completion target will be affected.

The Nakano Sun Plaza redevelopment became a symbol of rising construction costs when its plan was scrapped in 2025. The three-month delay itself is a small thing. What I'm watching are two points in the outline: the fixed-term land lease and the hall for about 3,000 people. Instead of selling the land and building on it, the ward leases the land and collects ground rent. For a developer, not having to pay for the land up front makes it easier to absorb rising construction costs. The hall, meanwhile, has gone from a maximum of 5,000 people in earlier studies to a baseline of about 3,000. When construction costs go up, the first things to get cut are the public parts that don't make much money. Reworking a plan also means deciding all over again what to keep and what to shrink. What would you want to survive in the new face of Nakano? On the way to the fiscal 2034 completion target, the revised plan in May 2027 is the next milestone.

Background
How the plan was scrapped

Nakano Sun Plaza closed in 2023. The redevelopment led by Nomura Real Estate Development and others saw its project cost balloon as construction costs soared, the application for approval was withdrawn in 2024, and the plan was scrapped in 2025. In March 2026 the ward ruled out renovating and reusing the existing building and set new targets of starting construction in fiscal 2030 and finishing in fiscal 2034.

Fixed-term land leases

A system in which a landowner leases land for a set period and the tenant builds on it. The ward earns ground rent without selling the land, while the developer keeps land acquisition costs down. What happens to the building when the lease ends is set out in the contract.

About 400 owners of three aging buildings in Kobe's Sannomiya pick RIA to coordinate a rebuild

A redevelopment council formed by the unit owners of three commercial buildings in Sannomiya, central Kobe, has chosen RIA to provide coordination services as it studies rebuilding. The method and timing are still undecided.

The Sun Center Plaza District Redevelopment Council, made up of the unit owners of three buildings on Sannomiya Center Gai, a covered shopping street in Sannomiya, the main downtown of the port city of Kobe, has chosen RIA for the redevelopment coordination contract it had put out to public tender. The buildings are San Plaza, Center Plaza and Center Plaza West, and the goal is to rebuild them. The construction trade papers Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun and Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun reported the selection on Oct 6. The work covers studies toward rebuilding and support for running the council, and the contract runs until March 31, 2027. RIA will also help build consensus toward setting up a redevelopment preparatory association.

San Plaza was completed in 1970, Center Plaza in 1975 and Center Plaza West in 1978, so all three are around half a century old. According to the Kobe Shimbun, there are about 400 unit owners, people who each own a section of a building much like condominium owners, and the City of Kobe is also one of them and sits on the council. The council was launched on July 24, 2026, following a study group on the buildings' future in 2021 and a rebuilding research group in 2024, and has now reached the stage of looking concretely at whether a rebuild is feasible.

The redevelopment method and schedule have not been decided, and the council is expected to spend several years putting together a draft basic plan. Rebuilding has not been formally decided. For now, the main goal is to settle the project framework and how existing property rights will be handled.

Anyone from Kobe knows these three commercial buildings on Sannomiya Center Gai. The hard part isn't how old they are. It's how many rights are involved. A coordinator's job, before drawing any design, is to map out a way to fit those 400 different situations into a single project. The revised Act on Building Unit Ownership, which took effect in April 2026, added a way to remove owners who can't be located from the count used for votes. Still, the real work of reaching consensus is people talking to each other. Is there a half-century-old building with many owners in your town too? This contract runs until the end of March 2027. I think the first thing to watch is how much of the project's framework is visible by then.

Background
Redevelopment coordinators

A consultant who, in the early stages of an urban redevelopment, gauges what the property owners want, studies how the project could be carried out, and helps set up a preparatory association. The role is different from the architect's or the builder's: the main work is building consensus and shaping the project framework. RIA is a design and consulting firm that has worked on many redevelopment plans.

The revised Unit Ownership Act (April 2026)

The revision created a system that lets owners who cannot be located be removed, with a court's approval, from the count used for votes. It also eased the requirements for votes to rebuild condominiums and similar buildings in certain cases, such as insufficient earthquake resistance. Commercial buildings are also covered by the law if they are divided into separately owned units.
