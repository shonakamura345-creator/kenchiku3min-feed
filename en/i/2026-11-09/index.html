Exhibition on Shigeru Ban's disaster relief in Japan's Noto Peninsula opens in Kanazawa

An exhibition on architect Shigeru Ban's disaster relief work in Oku-Noto, the northern tip of the Noto Peninsula on the Sea of Japan coast, opened on November 7 at the Yoshiro and Yoshio Taniguchi Museum of Architecture in Kanazawa.

The 14th special exhibition at the Yoshiro and Yoshio Taniguchi Museum of Architecture, Kanazawa, titled "Shigeru Ban and Oku-Noto: Years of Disaster Relief," opened on November 7 and runs until May 9, 2027. Kanazawa is the capital of Ishikawa Prefecture, the prefecture hit by the Noto Peninsula earthquake on New Year's Day 2024. Timed for the approach of the quake's third anniversary, the show features Ban, an architect whose mother's family comes from Kanazawa.

At its center is the relief work Ban and the Voluntary Architects' Network (VAN), the nonprofit he leads, have carried out in Oku-Noto. On display are the paper-tube partitions used in evacuation shelters, temporary housing built with stacked timber structural members so that people can keep living in it for the long term, temporary workshops that support traditional crafts such as Wajima lacquerware, and reconstruction buildings that reuse roof tiles and old timber.

The exhibition also looks back over Ban's path, from aid for Rwandan refugees in 1994 to the Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake of 1995 in Kobe, the Great East Japan Earthquake of 2011 and his work in Ukraine. Placing that history alongside Oku-Noto, it asks what role architecture plays in the region's recovery.

Briefing
  • The museum is in Teramachi 5-chome, Kanazawa, and is open from 9:30 a.m. to 5 p.m. (last entry 4:30 p.m.). It is closed on Mondays, or on the next weekday when Monday is a national holiday.
  • The museum was closed from October 19 to November 6 to change exhibitions, and this show opened as it reopened. The run is set fairly long, at just over half a year.
  • After the Noto Peninsula earthquake, Ban's office designed two-story wooden temporary apartment buildings near Mitsukejima, a rock island off the coast of Suzu, a city at the tip of the peninsula. The plan was to convert them into public housing for disaster victims after their use as temporary housing.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Frames made of paper tubes line a school gym used as a shelter, with fabric hanging from them. Mention Shigeru Ban and a lot of people picture exactly that, right? Paper tubes are the same cardboard cylinders you find inside a roll of toilet paper or plastic wrap. They're light, anyone can put them together, and they can be recycled when you're done. On a gym floor, they let you put up a wall of privacy right away. What I want to point out in this exhibition is that it goes beyond that stage. Shelter partitions, temporary housing, temporary workshops for traditional crafts. You can see, in a single venue, how architecture steps in one stage after another, as people evacuate, settle into a home, and get their work back. When I introduced Ban before, I talked about the question of who architecture is really for. Architecture supports lives and livelihoods, yet it's a job where that's hard to feel firsthand. Here you get half a year to see architecture helping people directly. That's the kind of exhibition this is, I think.

Background
From temporary to permanent

The wooden temporary housing Ban's office designed in Suzu after the Noto Peninsula earthquake was planned on the assumption that it would become public housing for disaster victims once its temporary use ended. Prefabricated temporary housing is usually removed after a few years, which doubles the work and cost of rebuilding. Making buildings that can last from the start is drawing attention as an option for housing in disaster areas.

How paper tubes began

Ban began using paper tubes in architecture in the 1980s, reportedly after trying them in place of wood for an exhibition design with a tight budget. The approach later spread to refugee camps and disaster areas in Japan and abroad, and when he won the Pritzker Prize in 2014, this social work was part of what was recognized.

Japan sets up cross-government task force on construction labor shortage to keep AI and chip plants on track

With too few workers to build factories and data centers, the Japanese government has launched a task force spanning several ministries. It will put together a policy package by the end of the year.

The government held the first meeting of its "task force on securing construction workers" on October 30. It is chaired by Deputy Chief Cabinet Secretary Masanao Ozaki, and its members include the secretariat of the Japan Growth Strategy Headquarters in the Cabinet Secretariat, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) and the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). At the Japan Growth Strategy Council on October 1, Prime Minister Takaichi had ordered a cross-government setup to tackle the construction labor shortage and a policy package by the end of the year.

Behind the move is the construction demand expected for factories and data centers in 17 strategic sectors, including AI and semiconductors. Clients who commission these projects say the shortage of construction workers is driving up costs and stretching schedules, holding back investment. At the first meeting, METI explained the issues on the client side and MLIT those on the contractor side.

The task force will work on three areas: measures to keep projects already underway moving, measures to train construction workers, and measures to make the work more efficient. Besides setting up a way to take consultations on individual projects, it plans to assemble a package by year-end that includes support for reskilling, wider eligibility for education and training benefits, and a review of the construction industry's retirement allowance mutual aid system.

Briefing
  • The task force sits under a liaison council of ministries and agencies that promotes training and securing workers in strategic sectors and fields related to social infrastructure. Its creation was decided at that council's meeting on October 16.
  • Government materials for the first meeting showed that, comparing the current state of building work by trade, the share of respondents calling conditions "somewhat tight" or "tight" was high in building services work. They added that many say the capacity of building services subcontractors is limited on semiconductor plant and data center projects.
  • Other items being considered for the package include wider use of flexible working-hour systems and making the work more efficient through AI and digital technology.

Every day, lots of tradespeople head to the construction site of a semiconductor plant. This task force means the government has started treating the shortage of those people as a problem for the country's growth strategy. Where it really bites is building services: electrical, air conditioning, water supply and drainage. If you compare a building to a human body, the structure is the skeleton and the services are the blood vessels and nerves. Chip plants and data centers are buildings where those vessels and nerves are exceptionally thick and complex, right? The government's own materials say that, by trade, complaints of tight labor are most common in building services work. What I keep wondering is what happens to the condos, schools and hospitals in our towns when workers gather at these big factories. The number of workers can't grow overnight. Who gets the limited people, for which jobs, and in what order? Is that decided by the market, or by the state? I wonder how far the year-end package will go into that.

Background
Two tracks of debate

On construction workers, MLIT's study group on a vision for the construction industry is also discussing changes such as moving skilled workers to monthly salaries. That is a medium- to long-term effort to change how the whole industry works, while this task force is a cross-government response aimed at keeping large investments in front of us from stalling.

No one to take the job

In Japan's big cities, a growing number of redevelopment projects are seeing their start delayed because no contractor can be found, with soaring construction costs and labor shortages to blame. If demand for large factory construction piles on top, competition for the limited pool of tradespeople and building services firms could get even fiercer.

Former department store in Essen, Germany, becomes central library as MVRDV turns six floors into a city living room

In Essen, in western Germany, a new central library created by converting a former department store opened on October 24. It was designed by the Dutch firm MVRDV and others.

Essen, a city in western Germany, opened its new central library on October 24. It occupies the Boeckerhaus, a former department store facing Kennedyplatz in the city center, and all six of its floors were converted into the library. The interior was designed by the Dutch firm MVRDV and includi, with the local firm Meyer Architekten collaborating.

The design is rooted in the idea of the "third place" put forward by sociologist Ray Oldenburg: somewhere that is neither home nor work, where anyone can drop by. The goal is to make the library something like the city's living room. Bookshelves are used like building blocks to divide the large floors into zones where different users spend their time in different ways.

Briefing
  • The library's floor area is given as about 10,000 m² (108,000 sq ft) in press reports and about 11,000 m² (118,000 sq ft) on MVRDV's official page, so the figure varies by source.
  • Until 2017 the Boeckerhaus housed the bookstore Mayersche, so it is a building with close ties to books.
  • The library used to be near the train station, and with this opening it has moved to Kennedyplatz in the city center.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Turn a closed department store into a library without tearing it down. Essen's new library keeps the building's outside and swaps out only what's inside. Here's the thing: department store buildings actually suit libraries well. Large floors stack up level after level, and escalators and stairs run through them on the assumption that crowds will move up and down. And they stand by the station or in the center of town, right where the most people gather, don't they? In other words, a box built for bringing people together is there from the start. In this design, those big floors are divided with bookshelves into places for quiet reading and places for livelier time. In Japan, too, more department stores in regional cities are closing and leaving large empty buildings in front of stations and in town centers. Tear it down and rebuild, or swap the contents and keep using it? One of those options has become six floors of bookshelves in the middle of a German city.

Background
Second lives for department stores

In Japan, department stores keep closing, especially in regional cities, and more large empty buildings are being left in town centers. Many are demolished for redevelopment, but some are kept in use with government service counters, libraries or places of learning. Buildings with large floors designed around the flow of people are considered a good fit for conversion to public facilities.
