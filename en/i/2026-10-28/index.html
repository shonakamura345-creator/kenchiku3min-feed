Update: Three months after its quake, Kumamoto sets deadlines for temporary homes and demolition

Kumamoto Prefecture in southwestern Japan has adopted a recovery and reconstruction plan for the 2026 Kumamoto earthquake. It aims to finish building temporary housing by December and to complete publicly funded demolition of damaged homes within next year.

Kumamoto Prefecture, on Kyushu, the southernmost of Japan's four main islands, adopted a recovery and reconstruction plan for the 2026 Kumamoto earthquake, which struck on July 28, at a meeting of its recovery headquarters on Oct 27. The plan sets out a three-year roadmap running through fiscal 2028 (Japan's fiscal year runs from April to March) and aims to restore daily life, the local economy and infrastructure within roughly three years. Oct 28 marks three months since the quake.

On housing, the prefecture set a goal of finishing construction of purpose-built temporary housing by December. It will also help municipalities complete "public-expense demolition", in which the local government demolishes and clears damaged homes on the owners' behalf, within next year. The prefecture says it will also give technical support to municipalities building public disaster-recovery housing, the permanent rental homes local governments provide for survivors. According to NHK, Japan's public broadcaster, evacuation shelters are to close by the end of the current fiscal year.

As we reported on Sept 29 and Oct 5, completion of temporary housing fell behind schedule, and residents began moving into two-story temporary units. The new plan is the first time the prefecture has laid out, with deadlines, a path from that emergency phase toward rebuilding permanent homes. The full text of the plan is scheduled to be posted on the prefecture's website on Oct 30.

Briefing
  • In its support package for the livelihoods and businesses of survivors of the 2026 Kumamoto earthquake, the national government calls for speeding up public-expense demolition by using a dedicated demolition support team. The prefecture's plan assumes this national support and is designed to back up the work of municipalities.
  • According to the Kumamoto Nichinichi Shimbun, the local daily newspaper, Kumamoto City covers buildings judged to be half destroyed or worse under public-expense demolition, at no cost to the owners. Owners apply, and the city carries out the demolition and clearance for them.
  • According to NHK, Yatsushiro, another city in the prefecture, also released a draft of its own recovery and reconstruction plan on the same day, Oct 27, looking ahead about 10 years to fiscal 2035. The prefecture's three-year roadmap and the municipalities' long-term plans are now moving forward side by side.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Temporary housing by December, public-expense demolition within next year. What I think matters most in this plan is the order of those two deadlines. Until a damaged house is cleared away, you can't build a new one on that land. It works the same way as moving house: you can't bring the new furniture in until the old furniture is carried out, can you? In fact, demolition is one of the stages of recovery where heavy machinery and labor tend to be fought over. There are only so many demolition contractors, and only so much room at the disposal sites that take the debris. When that bottleneck clogs, rebuilding homes as a whole slips back. In a broadcast at the end of July, I talked about examples of architects' ideas supporting daily life in disaster areas. But planning the steps from temporary units to real, lasting homes is just as much an architect's job. Today marks exactly three months since the earthquake.

Background
How public demolition works

Normally, the owner of a house damaged in a major disaster has to find a demolition contractor and pay to have it torn down. Under public-expense demolition, the municipality demolishes and clears the house on the owner's behalf, with costs covered by national subsidies and other funds. After the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake on Japan's Sea of Japan coast, a flood of applications and a shortage of contractors left owners waiting a long time for demolition, which became a problem because it delayed rebuilding.

From temporary to permanent homes

Under Japan's Disaster Relief Act, emergency temporary housing is provided for two years in principle. During that time, survivors either rebuild or repair their own homes, or, if they cannot rebuild on their own, move into public disaster-recovery housing built by local governments. After the 2016 Kumamoto earthquakes, too, building that public housing became a pillar of recovery once the temporary units were in place.

Worker run over and killed by heavy machinery at a building demolition site in Tokyo's Ginza

On Oct 26, a male worker who appeared to be in his 60s was run over by heavy machinery and killed at the demolition site of a six-story building in Ginza 1-chome, Tokyo. The Metropolitan Police Department is investigating the cause.

At around 4 p.m. on Oct 26, an emergency call came in to 119, Japan's number for fire and ambulance services, reporting that a person might be trapped between a building and heavy machinery at the demolition site of a six-story building in Ginza 1-chome, Chuo Ward, in Tokyo's best-known shopping district. According to the Metropolitan Police Department, Tokyo's police force, a man who appeared to be in his 60s and was working on the fifth floor is believed to have been run over by heavy machinery operated by another worker. He was pronounced dead at the scene.

According to the Asahi Shimbun, the machine was a hydraulic excavator, and the man was found collapsed in an area where demolished concrete and steel had been piled. At the time, three workers including the man were working on the fifth floor, and two people including the person in charge of the work were on the fourth floor. No other workers were injured. Police are investigating the detailed cause of the accident, including possible professional negligence resulting in death.

This is the third fatal accident at a construction or demolition site that we have reported this month, after steel material fell at a building construction site in Tokyo's Nihonbashi district (reported Oct 4) and an H-beam fell at a demolition site in Toyonaka, a city just north of Osaka (reported Oct 13). The contractor and the work procedure in use at the time have not been officially disclosed so far.

Briefing
  • TV stations reported the content of the emergency call as a person possibly trapped between a building and heavy machinery. The Asahi Shimbun reported that the man is believed to have been caught in the excavator's undercarriage, and police are examining where everyone was positioned at the time.
  • Police are working to confirm the identity of the man who died. The four other people working at the same site were not injured.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Some of you may have wondered why heavy machinery was up on the fifth floor. On tight sites in central Tokyo, a common method is to lift the machine onto the top of the building with a crane and take the building down one floor at a time, starting from the top. Think of a tower of toy blocks being taken apart one block at a time from the very top. In a place like Ginza, where there's no space at all around the building, you can't knock it down from outside with a big machine. That means machines and people end up moving within the same limited space on a single floor. Chunks of concrete and steel pile up underfoot, and blind spots from the operator's seat are easy to create. How this particular site was running its work hasn't been reported yet. With more and more old buildings being rebuilt, demolition work is only going to increase. I think now is exactly the time for everyone on site to rethink how much distance to keep between people and machines.

Background
Demolishing from the top down

When mid- and high-rise buildings are demolished, a small machine is often hoisted onto the roof to break down the floors in order from the top, lowering the debris through openings in the floors. The method is standard in central Tokyo, where sites have little spare room. But because the machine swings around on the floor while workers sort debris and spray water nearby, contact with the machine is considered a typical accident pattern.

Keeping people clear of machines

Japan's Industrial Safety and Health Regulations require employers using construction machinery such as excavators to keep people out of areas where they could come into contact with an operating machine, and to assign signal persons to guide it, among other measures. Each time a fatal accident involving contact with heavy machinery occurs, local labor standards inspection offices, the government's workplace safety inspectors, have asked the industry to carry out a general safety check of demolition sites.

Fukuoka's former Kyushu University campus will become HAKOZAKI BLOCK, a name that recalls its bricks

In the redevelopment of Kyushu University's former Hakozaki campus in Fukuoka, a group of eight companies led by Sumitomo Corporation has named the new district HAKOZAKI BLOCK. An electronics retailer and a supermarket are among the stores already lined up.

On Oct 22, an eight-company group led by Sumitomo Corporation announced that the redevelopment of Kyushu University's former Hakozaki campus, in Higashi Ward of Fukuoka, the largest city on the southern island of Kyushu, will be called HAKOZAKI BLOCK. Its concept is roughly "a town that opens up, all the way to your curiosity." The project is positioned as the lead project of FUKUOKA Smart EAST, a smart city initiative.

The word BLOCK carries several meanings: a city block, the bricks that carry on the memory of the Hakozaki campus, a shape that recalls the "hako" (box) in the name Hakozaki, and the image of toy building blocks. According to the Nishinippon Shimbun, a Fukuoka-based newspaper, the electronics retailer Edion, AEON Kyushu and Royal Home Center have been confirmed as anchor tenants of the commercial area.

The district plans a system that lets residents use services for shopping, transportation, health and more with a single shared ID, and aims to let people live without carrying keys or a wallet, getting in simply by showing their face. The first section is set to open in fiscal 2028, with development continuing in phases through fiscal 2036.

Briefing
  • At the proposal stage, the district's working name was HAKOZAKI GreenInnovation Campus. An official website, hakozaki-block.jp, has opened along with the announcement of the official project name.
  • The group of companies was formally chosen as the land-use developer by Kyushu University and the Urban Renaissance Agency (UR), Japan's public agency for housing and urban development. Besides Sumitomo Corporation, its members include Shimizu Corporation, Daiwa House Industry, Tokyu Land Corporation and the Nishinippon Shimbun.
  • The commercial area will include an electronics store, a supermarket and a home improvement store, so residents can do their everyday shopping within the district. Housing, education and medical facilities are also planned.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

The name BLOCK also carries the meaning of brick. After Kyushu University moved to its Ito campus in the city's Nishi Ward, a vast empty site was left behind here. Chances to design an area this large from scratch, right in the middle of a city, don't come along often. It's like being handed a blank sheet of graph paper and being free to draw in the streets and buildings. And what they've put forward is a town where your face is your pass. I'll admit, a life without keys or a wallet sounds pretty appealing, doesn't it? But from an architect's point of view, where you put the cameras and devices that read faces has a direct effect on the design of the streetscape and of building entrances. How much do we accept as convenience, and at what point do we start to feel watched? And who draws that line, the developers or the people who live there? Where would you draw it?

Background
Former campuses as a resource

When a university campus relocates, it is one of the few occasions when a large, single piece of land in a city becomes available all at once. Kyushu University completed its consolidated move to the Ito campus in 2018, and the future use of the Hakozaki site was debated for a long time afterward. The housing development on university land in Tokyo's Monzen-nakacho area, which we reported on Oct 20, is another example of a former university site being turned into part of the city.

Questions for smart cities

Town planning that links everyday services through a shared ID or face recognition is spreading in Japan, including Toyota's Woven City in Susono, Shizuoka Prefecture. As convenience grows, the same questions keep coming up everywhere: who manages the data collected, how, and how residents' consent is obtained.
