Update: Pritzker-winning architect Riken Yamamoto loses Yokohama mayoral race

Riken Yamamoto, 81, the 2024 Pritzker Prize laureate, lost the Yokohama mayoral election held on Oct 18. His campaign, built on guaranteeing the right to decent housing, fell short. "It was hard to win as an individual," he said.

Architect Riken Yamamoto, 81, has lost his bid to become mayor of Yokohama, the port city just south of Tokyo. In the election held on October 18, called after the previous mayor resigned, a 59-year-old lawyer running as an independent newcomer won a first term, beating six other candidates, including the former mayor. Yamamoto, who also ran as an independent and whose candidacy we reported on Oct 10, did not win. Turnout was 45.56%, up from 41.64% in the previous election.

Looking back on the campaign, Yamamoto told the Kanagawa Shimbun, the local newspaper: "I felt a real response, but party politics is strong. It was hard to win as an individual." The winner had been endorsed by the prefectural chapters of the Liberal Democratic Party, the Japan Innovation Party and the Democratic Party for the People, and was also backed by the Komeito party's group in the city assembly.

Yamamoto won the Pritzker Prize, often called the Nobel Prize of architecture, in 2024. He campaigned on "guaranteeing the right to live in safe and comfortable housing." He also put forward his idea of the "local community area" as city policy: a unit the size of a single neighborhood association that brings together childcare, support for older people, and government service counters.

Briefing
  • Voting took place on October 18. The official campaign began on October 4 with seven candidates: one former officeholder and six newcomers. The election was triggered by the resignation of the previous mayor over power harassment of city staff.
  • In a preliminary count by local broadcaster TV Kanagawa (as of 12:30 a.m. on the 19th, with 87.52% of votes counted), the winner had about 490,000 votes, far ahead of the rest of the field.
  • Yamamoto was born in 1945. His work in Kanagawa Prefecture, the prefecture that includes Yokohama, includes the Yokosuka Museum of Art. In 2025 that museum held a retrospective (its title translates as "Riken Yamamoto: Community and Architecture") that presented 50 years of his design work through models and drawings.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

One of seven candidates. That's how Riken Yamamoto's run for mayor ended. Judging the election itself isn't my job here. What I want to put on record, as an architect, is what he actually proposed. The "local community area" is about opening up homes that are usually closed off family by family, just a little, so that a few hundred neighbors can support each other's daily lives. Picture an apartment building whose ground floor works as a shared living room for all the residents. Or a small shop or workspace right next to each front door. Yamamoto has been proposing homes like that for years, in his buildings and in his books. Here was an architect presenting his theory not as drawings but as city policy. That alone is a rare thing. When he received the Pritzker Prize in 2024, what the jury recognized was exactly this stance: how to open up the boundary between the home and the community. Turnout this time was 45.56%. More than half of Yokohama's voters never went to the polls.

Background
Architects and politics

Architects shape what cities look like, but the decisions are made by their clients: governments and developers. Many architects take part in policymaking by sitting on municipal advisory councils or landscape committees, but running for mayor or governor is rare. Yamamoto's candidacy was an attempt to step out of the designer's role and change housing from the side of the rules themselves.

The idea of the threshold

Yamamoto has long emphasized what he calls the threshold (shikii): the in-between zone that sits between the private space inside a home and the public space outside. Think of the engawa, the veranda along a traditional Japanese house, or the doma, an earth-floored entry area, where you can meet people outside your family. His argument is that modern housing, in valuing privacy above all, has lost these in-between places.

Fuchu in western Tokyo plans 5,000-seat arena and library complex on former base land

The city of Fuchu in western Tokyo will move its municipal gym, now more than 50 years old, to former base land in the Sengencho area. The plan combines an arena for 5,000-plus people with a heated pool and a library.

The city of Fuchu, a suburb in the Tama area of western Tokyo, has outlined where its plans for a new main gym stand. According to the construction trade papers Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun and Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, the city expects a total floor area of 25,000 to 28,000 m² (270,000–300,000 sq ft). The idea is to put a main arena holding 5,000 or more people at the center, with a heated pool, a training room and a library all in one building.

The site is reserved land on the former Fuchu Base in the city's Sengencho area. The current municipal gym was built in 1971 and, after more than 50 years, is showing its age. In early 2026 the city completed a basic concept for the new gym, setting out a plan to relocate and rebuild it.

The city is now drawing up the basic plan, which will settle what exactly the facility contains. The Japan Research Institute, a think tank, is supporting the planning work. According to the reports, the city aims to compile an outline of the basic plan in May 2027 and a draft in June, and to finalize the plan from July onward.

Briefing
  • The reserved land on the former base, in Sengencho, covers about 14 hectares (about 35 acres). The city also plans a new park and a major road on the same former base site.
  • In its Oct 18 edition, Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun reported that Fuchu expects a complex including the arena and library of up to 28,000 m² (300,000 sq ft) in total floor area.
  • The city's basic concept positions the new gym as a multipurpose arena where people can also enjoy spectator sports, connected to the neighboring park.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

An arena for more than 5,000 people, and a library where you borrow books. Put them in the same building? Sounds like an odd pairing, doesn't it? But it's actually a well-thought-out combination. An arena is a building where crowds show up all at once, but only on game or concert days. On an ordinary weekday afternoon, the big lobby sits empty. Add a library, and students and older residents come in every day. It's the same thinking as a big supermarket by a Japanese train station that also has a bank counter and a branch office of city hall inside: you bring people with different purposes into one building, so there's always life in the place whenever you go. Fuchu's current gym dates from 1971. Over half a century, what we expect from a gym has widened from "a place to exercise" to "a place residents drop by every day." Out of up to 28,000 m² (300,000 sq ft), how much will go to the days with no game? If you were stopping by every day, what would you want to find there?

Background
Gyms turning into arenas

Across Japan, municipal gyms built from the mid-1960s to the mid-1980s are being replaced one after another. More and more often, the replacement is an arena that adds seating for watching, so it can host pro games and concerts as well as residents' own sports. The Yamato Kogyo Arena Himeji (about 5,000 people), which opened in October 2026 in Himeji, a city in Hyogo Prefecture west of Kobe, is part of the same trend.

Reusing former base land

The Tama area of western Tokyo still has a lot of land left behind when US military and Japan Self-Defense Forces facilities were returned. The former Fuchu Base is one example, and what to do with the reserved land the city acquired from the national government has been an open question for years. Such sites let a city use a large area at once, but they also require care for the natural environment, such as tree felling and protecting wildlife.

Design Museum Gent in Belgium reopens after four years, with a recycled-brick wing linking its historic buildings

Design Museum Gent in Belgium reopened on Oct 3. A new wing designed by British firm Carmody Groarke links the museum's buildings, including an 18th-century mansion.

Design Museum Gent, in the historic city of Ghent in northern Belgium, reopened on October 3 after more than four years of renovation and expansion. The new wing was designed by British architecture firm Carmody Groarke together with local Ghent firm ATAMA. The restoration of the historic buildings was handled by RE-ST.

The museum was originally a group of buildings, including an 18th-century mansion, the Hotel De Coninck, and an exhibition wing built in the late 20th century. The new wing slots in between them and ties the buildings together, with spaces for lectures, workshops and events. On the fourth floor there is an event space with views out over Ghent.

The new wing's exterior walls use bricks made from waste materials, developed specifically for this project. The reopened museum totals about 6,500 m² (70,000 sq ft), of which about 3,700 m² (40,000 sq ft) is exhibition space. Admission was free on the reopening weekend, October 3 and 4, and design events were held around the city until the 11th.

Briefing
  • Construction of the new wing began in 2023. Belgian public broadcaster VRT has described the new wing as a building that incorporates circular design thinking.
  • With the reopening, the museum launched CO-LAB, a program in which designers, researchers and local residents work together, using eight period rooms inside the mansion.
  • Director Katrien Laporte, who has led the museum, will step down on November 1, 2026, after seeing it reopen. Until a successor is chosen, two coordinators, one for projects and one for administration, will share the director's authority.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

An old mansion, a 20th-century exhibition wing, and a new wing. Buildings from different eras now connect in a single line. The hardest part of an addition like this is probably deciding how much the new wing should stand out. When you add a new building next to a historic one, designers usually agonize between two paths. Make it resemble the old building so it blends in, or make it look completely different so the two are clearly distinct. The Ghent wing chooses neither. It uses brick, the same material as the old streetscape. But those bricks are newly made from waste materials. From a distance, it fits into the city. Up close, you can tell it's new. It may be close to the feeling of laying new tatami, the woven straw mats on Japanese floors, in the same shade as the old ones in an old house. In Japan, too, plans to add new wings to museums and government buildings from the postwar Showa era are moving forward in many places. I think connecting to the past through materials is an approach we'll see chosen more and more.

Background
Two paths for additions

When adding to a historic building, architects have long debated whether to match the existing building's form and color or to contrast with it using glass or metal. International guidelines for restoring cultural heritage stress that newly added parts should be distinguishable later on. Sharing materials while showing newness is an attempt to find a middle path between the two.

From waste to building material

In Europe, efforts to turn demolition rubble and waste into new building materials are spreading. Because construction accounts for a large share of global carbon dioxide emissions, circulating materials instead of making them from scratch is becoming one of the criteria by which design is judged.
