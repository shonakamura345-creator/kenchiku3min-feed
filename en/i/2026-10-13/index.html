Worker killed by falling 1-ton steel H-beam at demolition site in Toyonaka, Osaka

On the morning of Oct 12, a steel H-beam about 5 m (16 ft) long and weighing about 1 ton fell during removal at a demolition site in Toyonaka, north of Osaka, pinning a 30-year-old worker. He was pronounced dead about four hours later.

A 30-year-old worker died after a steel H-beam fell on him at a demolition site in Toyonaka, a city in Osaka Prefecture, on the morning of Oct 12. Emergency services received a call shortly before 10 a.m. reporting that an H-beam had fallen and someone was trapped underneath. According to the Sankei Shimbun, the site was a facility for temporarily storing soil, and workers were using a crane to remove the H-beams that served as its columns and beams when one of them, about 5 m (16 ft) long and weighing about 1 ton, fell.

The man, a company employee from Wakayama City, was pinned by his upper body and was pronounced dead at the hospital about four hours later. MBS News reported that he suffered injuries to his cervical spine and spinal cord. The cause of the fall is not yet known. Police are investigating whether there were problems with the work procedures or the safety management system, including the possibility of professional negligence resulting in death.

In the demolition of a steel-frame building, columns and beams are cut free one at a time, then lifted by crane and lowered to the ground, over and over. Following the accident at a construction site in Tokyo's Nihonbashi district that we reported on Oct 4, this is the second fatal accident involving a falling object this month.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Five meters long, one ton. An H-beam, a steel member whose cross section is shaped like the letter H, is used for the columns and beams of steel-frame buildings. It is the skeleton of the building itself. In demolition, crews free these bones one by one, by torch-cutting or by removing bolts, then lift them out with a crane and lower them. Accidents tend to happen at two moments: the instant a member is cut free, and the moment a suspended load starts to swing. If the weight shifts before the cut is complete, the member moves in a direction no one planned for. And if the load weighs a ton, no person can stop it. That's why the iron rules on site are to never let anyone stand under a suspended load, and to have a certified slinger, the person who rigs the wire ropes, check the rigging before giving the signal. The cause is still unknown. But demolition sites carry a difficulty that construction sites don't: the members aren't always in the condition the drawings show, and rust or deformation makes it hard to read how the loads are flowing. Taking a building down safely takes just as much planning as putting one up. The man who died was 30 years old.

Background
Safety rules for demolition

For the demolition of steel-frame buildings, Japan's Ordinance on Industrial Safety and Health requires appointing an operations chief, drawing up a work plan, keeping people out from under suspended loads, and having certified slingers check the lifting gear. Falls from height are the leading cause of fatal accidents in Japan's construction industry, followed by objects falling or flying and structures collapsing.

A month of falling-object deaths

On Oct 2, steel material fell at an office building construction site in Tokyo's Nihonbashi district, killing a worker. The Toyonaka accident differs in that it happened on a demolition site rather than a construction site, and the member that fell was the very part being removed. Demolition work is increasing as construction investment grows and buildings age, and building systems to prevent accidents has become a pressing issue.

Kishiwada picks Ishimoto to design new city hall after failed design-build bid, aiming for 2032

Kishiwada, a city in southern Osaka Prefecture, has selected Ishimoto Architectural & Engineering Firm to design its new city hall. After a design-build call drew no bidders in fiscal 2025, the city split design and construction into separate contracts.

The city of Kishiwada in Osaka Prefecture has chosen Ishimoto Architectural & Engineering Firm (its Osaka office) as the designer of its new city hall through an open proposal process, a common Japanese way of selecting a designer by evaluating their approach rather than a finished design. The Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun, a construction industry newspaper, reported the decision on Oct 13. Four firms took part: Ishimoto, Yasui Architects & Engineers, Sokikaku Sekkei and Yamashita Sekkei. The new building is planned at about 17,500 m² (188,000 sq ft) of total floor area including bicycle parking, with the goal of opening around fiscal 2032.

The city originally planned to use design-build (DB), in which design and construction are awarded to a single contractor. But its call for bidders in fiscal 2025 drew no applicants, so it switched to awarding design and construction separately. According to the city's public notice, the price cap for the design proposals was about ¥741.8 million, with the contract running until Dec 28, 2028.

Rebuilding Kishiwada's city hall has been a long, winding process. In a 2020 design-build proposal competition, four outside jury members resigned over the city's decision to disqualify two bidders, and in March 2021 the city council voted down the construction contract. A fiscal 2024 proposal competition was also canceled after participants withdrew. The current city hall consists of an old wing completed in 1954 and a new wing completed in 1971. A seismic assessment gave the new wing an Is value, the seismic index used in Japanese structural assessments, of 0.21, far below the level required for a disaster response base.

Briefing
  • Four firms entered the proposal process: Ishimoto Architectural & Engineering Firm, Yasui Architects & Engineers, Sokikaku Sekkei and Yamashita Sekkei. The design price cap was ¥741,799,000 including tax, and the contract deadline is Dec 28, 2028
  • The current city hall is made up of five buildings: the old wing (1954), the new wing and staff hall (1971), an annex (1982) and a second annex (1994). A seismic assessment carried out after the 1995 Great Hanshin-Awaji (Kobe) Earthquake gave the new wing an Is value of 0.21
  • The basic plan has been revised repeatedly. The 2019 version called for about 15,400 m² (166,000 sq ft) at an estimated ¥13.16 billion, and the 2022 version about 13,400 m² (144,000 sq ft) at ¥9.99 billion. The fiscal 2025 version moved the site to next to the city's welfare center and raised the floor area to about 17,500 m² (188,000 sq ft)
  • The 2020 design-build proposal was advertised with a cap of ¥12.705 billion. Four outside jury members resigned over the disqualification of two bidders, and in March 2021 the city council rejected the construction contract

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

An Is value of 0.21. This is an index of how well a building stands up to earthquakes, and buildings that serve as disaster response bases are expected to reach 0.9 or higher. In Kishiwada, the city hall, which is supposed to be the command center in a disaster, has been one of the weakest buildings in the city for more than 20 years. The reason it still wasn't rebuilt isn't a technical one. It's a procurement problem. Design-build, where you hand both design and construction to one party, shortens the schedule and lets you use the contractor's expertise from an early stage. But when construction costs are rising, contractors can't take on the price risk, so they stop raising their hands. Zero applicants in fiscal 2025 is a textbook case of that, I think. The cultural arts hall in Tokushima, a city on the island of Shikoku that I talked about on my show, also had its plan scrapped after a designer had been chosen and detailed design was underway. With public buildings, if you don't design how you're going to commission the building before you design the building itself, you can lose years. This time, Kishiwada went back to the most orthodox approach: separate design and construction, and pick the designer first. It may look like a detour, but I think it's the surest path.

Background
Design-build versus separate contracts

Design-build (DB) awards design and construction to a single company, with the advantages of a shorter schedule and buildability built into the design. But because the contractor carries the risk of cost changes from the design stage on, it attracts fewer bidders when materials and labor costs are rising. Separating design and construction takes longer, but it lets the client choose a designer first, settle the design, and then pick a builder through competition.

City halls across Japan

Many local government buildings built around the 1970s lack seismic strength and are due for replacement, but soaring construction costs are causing failed bids and plan revisions around the country. Otsu, in Shiga Prefecture, is moving ahead with basic design under the conventional approach, while Kamakura canceled its basic design contract to start over. One city after another is rethinking how it commissions these projects.

A year after Expo 2025, Yoichi Ochiai's null² pavilion lives on as a permanent theater in Yokohama

null²ⁿ, a permanent theater museum that reworks the experience of null², a Signature Pavilion at Expo 2025 Osaka, Kansai, opens on Oct 14 in Yokohama Landmark Tower.

null²ⁿ (pronounced "nurunuru nexus"), a permanent theater museum produced by media artist Yoichi Ochiai, has its grand opening on Oct 14 on the fifth floor of Landmark Plaza, the shopping mall in Yokohama Landmark Tower. Yokohama is a port city just south of Tokyo. The facility carries on the experience of null² ("nurunuru"), one of the Signature Pavilions at Expo 2025 Osaka, Kansai. According to Tokyo Art Beat, the project does not recreate the Expo building but reworks the experience as a permanent theater.

The facility consists of a "gate" and five areas, and a visit takes about 60 minutes. Along with two LED theaters, it adds a new anechoic space designed for focusing on sound, and Area 3 displays about 170 human inventions. Admission is by advance reservation for a set date and time, with adult tickets starting at ¥3,900 on weekdays.

At the Expo, null² was designed by architect Keisuke Toyoda of NOIZ and drew attention as a "dynamic architecture" whose exterior moved, combining a mirrored membrane that resonated with the wind and robotic arms. Expo 2025 closed exactly one year ago, on Oct 13, 2025. As a case of a pavilion's legacy being carried on as a permanent facility, it is getting attention from the architecture world as well.

Briefing
  • Opens Wednesday, Oct 14, on the fifth floor of Landmark Plaza in Yokohama Landmark Tower. Advance reservation for a set date and time is required, and adult tickets start at ¥3,900 on weekdays (PR TIMES)
  • Made up of a gate and five areas, taking about 60 minutes. Two LED theaters and an anechoic space, with about 170 human inventions on display in Area 3
  • The Expo's null² was produced by Yoichi Ochiai, with architectural design by NOIZ (Keisuke Toyoda), facade development by Taiyo Kogyo and structural engineering by Arup. Expo 2025 Osaka, Kansai closed on Oct 13, 2025

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Expo pavilions are built on the assumption that they'll be torn down after six months. That's what allows bold structures and facades, but it also means that when the Expo closes, the experience disappears along with the building. What makes null²ⁿ interesting is that instead of relocating the building, it pulled out only the "experience" and fit it back into the fifth floor of a shopping mall. At the Expo, null² was a pavilion where the building itself was like a machine, with a mirrored membrane moved by robotic arms. In Yokohama, that moving facade is gone. Instead, the experience inside is rebuilt with LED theaters and an anechoic room, a room where sound reflections are eliminated. As an architect, I'm a little sad that the mirrored facade didn't survive, but if you're going to make it permanent, keeping the experience rather than the building is a realistic call. In April, I saw Nuru-an, a piece Ochiai made in a six-tatami room, a small Japanese room sized by its floor mats, at the TOKYOROOMS exhibition in Toranomon, Tokyo. The idea of letting noise and silence share the same space is surely carried on in Yokohama too. Which Expo pavilion experience stuck with you the most? If you ever get the chance to visit Yokohama, try listening to your own heartbeat in the anechoic room.

Background
The legacy of Expo pavilions

Expo pavilions are generally demolished after the event closes, but some survive, like the Tower of the Sun from the 1970 Osaka Expo, and in other cases parts of buildings or exhibits are reused elsewhere. For Expo 2025, reuse of the timber from the Grand Ring, the huge wooden roof walkway around the site, and relocation or reuse of individual pavilions are being considered. null²ⁿ is a different kind of legacy: making the experience permanent.

null² as architecture

null² was designed by Keisuke Toyoda of NOIZ. It was made of stacked cubes measuring 2 m (6.6 ft) and 4 m (13 ft) on a side, wrapped in a mirrored membrane moved by robotic arms. Taiyo Kogyo developed the facade and Arup handled the structure. Billed as "architecture as an interface" connecting the physical and the digital, it was one of the most talked-about pavilions at the Expo.
