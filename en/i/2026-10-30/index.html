DESIGNART TOKYO opens on Ginza's KK Line, a decommissioned elevated road in Tokyo

One of Japan's largest design and art festivals opened today. For its 10th edition, the main venue sits on top of the KK Line, an elevated road in Tokyo's Ginza district that was closed to cars last year.

The design and art festival DESIGNART TOKYO 2026 opened on October 30. For its 10th edition, the main venue is the former Tokyo Expressway, known as the KK Line, an elevated road above Ginza, Tokyo's upscale shopping district, that ended its role as a road for cars in April 2025. An exhibition space called AUTOBAHN / KK Line has been set up on top of the elevated structure, running from near the Sukiyabashi intersection to Ginza 1-chome.

The main venue was designed by ALTEMY, the practice led by architect Eri Tsugawa. It is made up of four areas: exhibitions and installations, a pop-up design store, a stage for live jazz and talks, and a cafe. The main venue is open for five days through November 3, and a ticket is required.

The KK Line is an elevated road that, since its closure, has been the subject of a plan to turn it into a space centered on pedestrians. The festival gives people a chance to actually walk on it and spend time there before it takes its finished form.

Briefing
  • The main venue entrance is next to the Tokyo Kotsu Kaikan building, on the north side of Ginza Inz 2. The nearest stations include Yurakucho, Ginza and Ginza-itchome, all within a five-minute walk.
  • The festival as a whole runs through November 8, with about 150 participants from 13 countries at about 70 venues across Tokyo. Exhibitions outside the main venue are held at each of those venues.
  • Tickets are sold on ArtSticker. A one-day ticket costs ¥3,500 for general admission (¥3,000 in advance), and a five-day pass costs ¥6,000 (¥5,000 in advance). This year's theme is “Hibiki.”
  • The Tokyo Metropolitan Government and Tokyo Expressway Co. also held an event on the KK Line on April 25 and 26, 2026, Roof Park Fes & Walk 2026, to let people experience the future pedestrian space.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Starting today, people are walking on top of an elevated road built for cars. The KK Line is a road that has run through Ginza as if it were linking the rooftops of the buildings. Cars stopped using it last April, and it's now partway through being prepared for a new life as a space for pedestrians. Here's the thing: moments like this, when you get to use a place once before it's finished, are incredibly valuable for design. On drawings, where the shade falls and how the wind moves through can only ever be predictions, right? But once real people show up, you can clearly see where they stop and where they want to sit. It's a bit like spending a night in an empty room before deciding where the furniture goes. The venue was designed by ALTEMY, the practice led by architect Eri Tsugawa. It divides the long, narrow space of the elevated road into four parts: exhibitions, a shop, a stage, and a cafe. If you're ever in Tokyo walking around Ginza, look up near Sukiyabashi at the elevated road that crosses above the street.

Background
From roadway to walkway

Car traffic on the KK Line ended on April 5, 2025, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government has set out a concept to turn it into a pedestrian-centered space called the Tokyo Sky Corridor. The idea is to add benches and plantings and create small plazas, and the plan is reported to open it in stages, starting with some sections. Events like this one also serve as a way to test how the space gets used before full-scale construction.

Elevated parks before it

The best-known example of turning an unused elevated structure into a place to walk is New York's High Line, built on a former freight rail line. In Japan, too, cities across the country face the question of how to give old road and rail structures back to the city, and in Nihonbashi, a section of the Shuto Expressway, Tokyo's elevated urban highway, is being moved underground. Ginza's KK Line is an example of that movement taking shape in the very heart of the city.

Shibuya unveils draft plan for three towers up to 180 m in Tokyo's Sakuragaoka district

Shibuya Ward has released a draft plan to redevelop the Sakuragaoka district southwest of Shibuya Station with three high-rise buildings. Construction is to start in fiscal 2030, with completion in fiscal 2036.

Shibuya Ward has released a draft city planning proposal for the redevelopment of the Sakuragaoka district, on the southwest side of Shibuya Station in Tokyo. The area covers about 2.2 hectares (5.4 acres) and will be developed in three blocks, A, B and C. Total floor area comes to about 243,200 m² (2.6 million sq ft), with construction set to start in fiscal 2030 and completion targeted for fiscal 2036. (Japan's fiscal year runs from April to March.)

Block A, the largest, is planned to have a building with 35 floors above ground and two below, about 180 m (590 ft) tall, housing offices, shops and a hall. Block B would have a 17-story building about 90 m (300 ft) tall with educational facilities, shops and parking, and Block C a 26-story building about 120 m (390 ft) tall with housing, offices and a hotel, among other uses.

The ward is accepting public comments on the draft until November 9. This month it has been holding public input sessions both online and in person, and the in-person session is scheduled for tonight, October 30, at the Shibuya Cultural Center Owada.

Briefing
  • The ward's website has posted the draft documents, including a comment card, the draft city plan, a comparison table of the old and new plans, and a draft of the Type 1 urban redevelopment project, a Japanese scheme in which landowners pool their plots to build a larger complex.
  • The in-person input session is scheduled for Friday, October 30, from 6:30 to 8 p.m. in Study Room 1 on the second floor of the Shibuya Cultural Center Owada.
  • The three buildings are about 180 m (590 ft) tall in Block A, about 120 m (390 ft) in Block C and about 90 m (300 ft) in Block B, stepping down in height the farther they are from the station.

Background
Sakuragaoka's redevelopment so far

In Sakuragaoka, the redevelopment of the Sakuragaoka-guchi district closest to the station produced Shibuya Sakura Stage, which was completed on November 30, 2023, and officially opened as a neighborhood in summer 2024. The district in the new draft continues to its southwest and is the next stage. Renewal is moving step by step from the station up the slope.

Big projects around Shibuya

The area around Shibuya Station has seen one high-rise project after another, including Shibuya Scramble Square and redevelopment on the Dogenzaka side. Among them, the Sakuragaoka draft is a long-range plan, with completion not until fiscal 2036. Combining housing and educational facilities with offices sets it apart from the big buildings right at the station.

Zaha Hadid's former London studio, a converted school, opens to the public on November 3

The Zaha Hadid Foundation has converted the London office Hadid used for more than 30 years into a cultural venue. It opens to the public on November 3, marking 10 years since her death.

The Zaha Hadid Foundation, the foundation of the late architect Zaha Hadid, has renovated her former office in Clerkenwell, London, into a cultural venue that anyone can visit, and will open it to the public on November 3. A press preview was held on October 29. This year marks 10 years since Hadid died.

The building is a former Victorian-era school that is listed as a historic building in the UK, and Hadid's office used it for more than 30 years. The renovation was carried out by the London design practice KA, adding galleries, a lecture hall and spaces for learning.

The opening exhibition, “Zaha Hadid: Architecture of Performance,” focuses on the stage sets and stage designs Hadid created. It runs until March 27, 2027, and admission is free.

Briefing
  • The venue is open Tuesday to Saturday from 11 a.m. to 6 p.m. and closed on Sundays and Mondays. Its address is 10 Bowling Green Lane, London.
  • To mark the opening, the foundation, the architecture school AA School (Architectural Association) and the Serpentine Gallery are jointly holding a two-day symposium, “89° of Zaha Hadid.”
  • Besides exhibitions, the venue plans programs such as workshops and residencies.
  • There is no elevator to the upper floors of the building; they can only be reached by narrow stairs.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

When you hear the name Zaha Hadid, I imagine many of you picture bold buildings made almost entirely of curves. In Japan, some people may have first learned her name from her original design for Tokyo's New National Stadium, the one that was never built. Yet the place she used as her base for more than 30 years was an old Victorian school. The studio that produced those futuristic forms was actually a school building more than 100 years old. This renovation, too, doesn't paint over the building to make it look new. It keeps the look of the listed building while making the inside usable. There are only narrow stairs to the upper floors, and no elevator. Keeping an old building means taking on its inconveniences along with it, doesn't it? What I also find interesting is that the first exhibition is about stage design, not buildings. It doesn't sum up an architect's work through finished buildings alone. I think that's a fitting choice for the 10th anniversary of her death.

Background
Keeping architects' workplaces

Offices and homes once used by architects are open to the public in many parts of the world, and many people visit them. Preserving the place where design actually happened can show how the work was done in a way the finished buildings alone cannot. This venue, too, is planned not only for exhibitions but as a place for research and learning.

Zaha and Japan

Zaha Hadid won the 2012 international design competition for Tokyo's New National Stadium, the main venue planned for the Tokyo Olympics, but the plan was scrapped in 2015, partly because of soaring construction costs. The episode made her name widely known in Japan, and it also sparked debate over how large public buildings should be handled.
