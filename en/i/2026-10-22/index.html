Shiga's preferred plan keeps its 87-year-old prefectural office whole and adds a new wing

At an advisory panel meeting on Oct 19, Shiga Prefecture compared four layout plans for its government buildings and rated as most effective the one that keeps the Main Building and adds a new wing of about 43,200 m² (465,000 sq ft).

Shiga Prefecture, which surrounds Lake Biwa, Japan's largest lake, just east of Kyoto, has compared four ways to lay out its government office complex. At the fifth meeting of an advisory panel on the future of the prefectural government buildings, held Oct 19 at the prefectural office in Otsu, the prefecture's capital, it presented the results. It rated as most effective the plan that demolishes the East Building, scales back parts of the New Building (Shinkan) and the even newer Shin-Shinkan, and adds a new wing with a total floor area of about 43,200 m² (465,000 sq ft).

The Main Building, a national Registered Tangible Cultural Property, would be preserved in its entirety. Completed in 1939, it is a four-story reinforced concrete building designed by Koichi Sato, who designed many prefectural government buildings, and Hiroshi Kunieda, known for his architectural ornament. In 2014 it became the fifth prefectural headquarters still in use in Japan to be listed as a Registered Tangible Cultural Property, a lighter tier of the country's national heritage system.

The panel began in September 2025 and has been gathering views from academics and residents. According to news reports, it will compile a draft report in January, and the prefecture expects to set out its redevelopment policy based on that report before the end of this fiscal year, which in Japan runs through March.

Briefing
  • The panel is chaired by Professor Setsuko Nakajima of Kyoto University's Graduate School of Human and Environmental Studies. Members discussed the functions the offices need as well as how the plan relates to the national government's regional joint office building, which houses local branches of national ministries.
  • The Main Building was built by Obayashi Corporation. The prefecture points to the terracotta ornament on the central staircase and the interiors of the governor's office and the assembly chamber as highlights. Construction began in September 1937 and was completed in May 1939.
  • According to news reports, any of the plans would take more than 10 years to carry out. Construction cost estimates differ from one news outlet to another, so the prefecture's official policy is the thing to watch.

The Main Building of the Shiga prefectural government in Otsu still has terracotta ornaments, decoration made of fired clay, on its central staircase. It was finished in 1939, as steel was growing scarce during the Second Sino-Japanese War, which makes it one of the last large buildings of the prewar era. Now the prefecture has rated keeping that entire building and putting a big new wing next to it as the best option. What I want to focus on is how it's being kept. When old buildings are saved, it's not unusual for only the front wall to survive, pasted onto a new building. This time the direction is to preserve the whole building. Think of a family home: you keep living in the main house from your grandfather's day and rebuild the outbuildings around it. That's the picture, right? In exchange, the East Building comes down, and the New Building and the Shin-Shinkan are partly cut back. They're separating what to protect from what to renew and reorganizing the whole complex. The new wing: about 43,200 m² (465,000 sq ft). The prefecture's policy comes out this fiscal year.

Background
Prewar prefectural offices

Shiga's Main Building is the fifth prefectural headquarters still in use in Japan to be registered as a cultural property. For local governments that keep using prewar office buildings, a shared challenge is protecting their heritage value while dealing with earthquake resistance and a lack of space.

Choosing to shrink

As Japan's population falls and government services go digital, reducing floor area instead of continually adding to buildings, known in Japanese as genchiku, has become an option. This plan follows the same thinking: it sorts out a cluster of buildings that grew through repeated additions and gathers the functions that are needed into a new wing.

Narita Airport pushes back its new runway to the end of 2034 as land purchases stall

Narita International Airport Corporation said on Oct 19 it will move the completion target for its new Runway C and related work from March 2029 to the end of December 2034. Land acquisition has stalled, and the company will also start land expropriation procedures.

Narita International Airport Corporation (NAA), which runs the airport in Chiba Prefecture east of Tokyo, announced on Oct 19 that it had notified the Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism of a new completion date for Runway C and related facilities: Dec 31, 2034. The previous target was March 31, 2029, so the schedule slips by five years and nine months.

Runway C, 3,500 m (11,500 ft) long, is the centerpiece, along with an extension of the existing Runway B, of a plan to raise Narita's annual capacity to 500,000 takeoffs and landings. According to news reports, about a tenth of the land for Runway C has yet to be acquired. NAA will keep negotiating with landowners while also pursuing procedures under Japan's land expropriation system, which allows land to be acquired compulsorily, similar to eminent domain.

Expropriation requires a project certification from the national government. Before applying, NAA plans to hold legally required briefing sessions starting Oct 28, in turn in the city of Narita and the towns of Tako and Shibayama. Meanwhile, the Runway B extension, for which nearly all the land has been secured, aims to open first, within fiscal 2029.

Briefing
  • The end-of-2034 date is the target for finishing construction; no date has been given for Runway C to enter service. According to news reports, Runway C and a new cargo area are aiming to open in the early 2030s.
  • Narita's expansion got fully underway in January 2020, when the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) approved changes to the airport's facilities. Runway B will be extended from 2,500 m (8,200 ft) to 3,500 m (11,500 ft).
  • The expropriation briefings will be held in three municipalities: Narita, Tako and Shibayama. Even after the project is certified, several more steps are required, including hearings by an expropriation committee.

Moving into expropriation procedures now, running alongside the talks, is a major turning point. The expansion of an airport, and the land of the people who have lived there. Who gets to decide where the two meet?

Background
Narita's history

Japan's cabinet decided to build Narita Airport in 1966, but fierce local opposition delayed its opening until 1978. Afterward, the national government took a path of negotiation instead of relying on compulsory expropriation, and Runway B stayed shorter than planned for a long time.

An airport expansion race

Tokyo's other airport, Haneda, began using new flight routes over central Tokyo in 2020, and the number of takeoff and landing slots in the Tokyo area keeps growing. Major Asian airports are also adding runways, and Narita's expansion was planned amid that international competition.

Japan's custom-home costs rise for a 14th straight year to ¥72.93 million as homes shrink

In a fiscal 2025 survey by the Japan Federation of Housing Organizations, the average construction cost of a custom-built home was ¥49.54 million and the total cost including land hit a record ¥72.93 million. Average floor area shrank to 118.9 m² (1,280 sq ft).

The Japan Federation of Housing Organizations (Judanren), an industry group of major homebuilders and others, has compiled its fiscal 2025 survey of people who built custom detached homes, houses built to the owner's order rather than bought ready-made. In Japan's major metropolitan areas, the average construction cost was ¥49.54 million, up ¥1.94 million from the previous year. The total cost including land rose for the 14th year in a row to ¥72.93 million, a record high.

Homes, meanwhile, are getting smaller. Average floor area was 118.9 m² (1,280 sq ft), down 3.6 m² (39 sq ft) from 122.5 m² (1,320 sq ft) the year before, dipping below 120 m² (1,290 sq ft). Floor area has been shrinking since fiscal 2015. Asked how they responded to rising prices, 51.3% said they reduced the size of their home, more than half of respondents.

This was the 26th edition of the survey, with 2,709 valid responses. The federation believes that as construction costs rise, buyers are coping by keeping floor area down while borrowing more.

Briefing
  • Of the 2,709 valid responses, 2,560 were collected through the federation's member companies. The federation is an industry group made up of major homebuilders and others.
  • In the previous survey, for fiscal 2024, the average construction cost was ¥47.6 million. That means it rose by about ¥2 million in a year.
  • The total cost of acquiring a home rose ¥2.87 million in a year. The federation's analysis is that because there are limits to how much buyers can increase their own savings or gifts from parents, they are increasingly making up the difference with loans.

Half of the people who built a custom home said they cut its size because of rising prices. Average floor area was 118.9 m² (1,280 sq ft). That's roughly 36 tsubo, the traditional Japanese unit of about 3.3 m² (35.6 sq ft) still used for homes, and it has finally slipped under 120 m² (1,290 sq ft). This year's drop was 3.6 m² (39 sq ft). That's a little over two tatami mats, the straw mats Japanese rooms are measured in, or exactly one storage room or a roomy walk-in closet gone. But I don't think a smaller home is a bad thing in itself. You know those small cafes that somehow feel really comfortable, right? How happy you are with a home isn't decided by size alone either. Cut down on hallways, build storage into the walls, use ceiling height and window placement to make rooms feel bigger. There's still plenty design can do. What worries me is that the total cost, building plus land, has gone up 14 years in a row. There's a limit to how much of that increase you can absorb by trimming space. Build small, live well. I think custom homes have entered that kind of era.

Background
Small-house tricks

Ways to make a home feel bigger without adding floor area include layouts with fewer hallways, double-height voids, and skip floors, which shift floor levels against each other. The higher the construction cost per square meter, the more thought each square meter demands.

The weight of mortgages

As we reported on Oct 1, major banks raised their variable mortgage rates in October. The more you borrow, the harder rising rates hit, and that also shapes how buyers decide on home size and budget.
