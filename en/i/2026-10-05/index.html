Update: Kumamoto's first two-story temporary housing opens after quake, allowing vertical flood evacuation

On Oct 4, residents began moving into the Shinmura temporary housing complex in the town of Hikawa, the first two-story temporary housing built in Kumamoto Prefecture. Built in response to a shortage of land, it fits about 1.5 times as many units as single-story housing on the same area.

In Hikawa, a town in Kumamoto Prefecture on the southern island of Kyushu that was hit by the 2026 Kumamoto earthquake, residents began moving on Oct 4 into the Shinmura temporary housing complex, the prefecture's first two-story purpose-built temporary housing. According to the Kumamoto Nichinichi Shimbun, the light-gauge steel, two-story buildings have 13 units, housing 34 people. All of the temporary housing built so far, including the town's Yoshimoto complex we reported on Sept 5, has been single-story.

The reason for going two-story was a lack of land. There is little flat land for building in the town, and according to NHK, going two-story allows about 1.5 times as many units as single-story housing on the same amount of land. The site is in a zone mapped as at risk of flooding, where residents are asked to evacuate in advance during heavy rain, but the layout also lets residents evacuate vertically to the second floor if flooding does occur.

Kumamoto Prefecture plans to build 700 purpose-built temporary housing units in all, 88 of them two-story, and construction is also under way in the city of Yatsushiro. The prefecture is also offering another form of temporary housing for disaster victims who find it hard to leave home because of work, placing container houses or similar units on their own property. Temporary housing is taking on more varied forms.

Briefing
  • The Shinmura complex in Hikawa's Miyahara area is the town's fourth temporary housing complex. The 62 units in the three earlier complexes are all single-story, and these 13 units are the first two-story ones in the prefecture (Kumamoto Nichinichi Shimbun)
  • Kumamoto Prefecture began building the two-story units on Aug 13. Of the 700 purpose-built temporary units planned across the prefecture, 88 are two-story, and more are being built in Yatsushiro (NHK)
  • From Oct 1, the city of Kumamoto has been seeking additional residents for 14 units at three temporary complexes in its Minami Ward, with move-in planned for Oct 17. In the town of Kosa, 20 units in seven single-story buildings are aiming for move-in to begin in early October

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Temporary housing has gone two-story. You might think, is that all? But purpose-built temporary housing is legally an “emergency” building. It's meant to go up fast and, in principle, come down after two years, so rows of single-story prefabs have been the standard for a long time. The reason for going two-story this time is that there's no land. In a town with little flat land, the difference of fitting 1.5 times as many units on the same area translates directly into the number of families waiting to move in. Another thing I'm paying attention to is that this site is inside a zone at risk of flooding. It's temporary housing for an earthquake, but the next thing to hit might be heavy rain. With a second floor, residents can evacuate vertically. I've also talked on my show about architect Shigeru Ban's paper partitions for evacuation shelters: architecture in a disaster has to deliver speed, safety and numbers all at once. I think this means lining up single-story units is no longer the only answer. Kumamoto Prefecture plans 700 temporary units. Of those, 88 are two-story.

Background
Built versus leased

Emergency temporary housing comes in two types: purpose-built units such as prefabs, and “deemed temporary housing,” in which local governments rent vacant private apartments. Under the Disaster Relief Act, housing is provided for two years in principle. Securing land is a constant problem for purpose-built housing, and land shortages delayed move-ins after both the 2016 Kumamoto earthquake and the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake.

Temporary housing diversifies

After the Noto Peninsula earthquake, wooden row-house units and a “hometown return” type that can later be converted into public housing were introduced, and two-story temporary housing was also used in some places. Temporary housing design is being updated with each disaster, moving from single-story units meant to be removed toward forms shaped by site conditions and future use.

Today, Oct 5, is World Architecture Day, with the theme “Housing is a Human Right. Build It.”

World Architecture Day, set by the International Union of Architects (UIA), falls on the first Monday of October, which is Oct 5 this year. Under the 2026 theme, “Housing is a Human Right. Build It.” the UIA is calling on architects to get involved in the housing crisis.

Today, Oct 5, is World Architecture Day, established by the International Union of Architects (UIA), the global federation of architects' organizations. The UIA created the day in 1985, and it is held every year on the first Monday of October, the same day as the United Nations' World Habitat Day. The theme for 2026 is “Housing is a Human Right. Build It.” The UIA has also scheduled an online webinar for the day.

The UIA describes the housing crisis as one of the most pressing challenges facing cities and communities around the world and argues that access to adequate housing should be recognized as a basic right. It calls on architects to go beyond design work and join debates on housing policy and affordability, and to pursue practical, sustainable approaches such as developing inclusive housing models and reusing existing buildings.

This year's theme for the UN's World Habitat Day is “Adequate Housing for All,” so the two are in step. In Japan, there is no shortage of housing in sheer numbers, but as we reported on Oct 1, starts of owner-occupied homes are sluggish, and rising interest rates and construction costs are narrowing people's housing choices.

Briefing
  • The UIA created World Architecture Day in 1985. Since 1996 it has been held on the same day as the UN's World Habitat Day (the first Monday of October), with a theme set each year and architects' organizations in many countries holding events
  • The UIA's message rests on three points: housing is a right, not a luxury; architects should go beyond design and take part in debates on housing policy and affordability; and inclusive housing models should be developed and the existing building stock reused (UIA)
  • The theme of World Habitat Day 2026 is “Adequate Housing for All.” UN-Habitat is also promoting World Architecture Day events on the same day

When someone says “housing is a human right,” it may sound a bit distant in Japan. Something for countries where lots of people live on the street, you might think. But I believe Japan has its own housing problem. As I said on today's show, the average age of homes when they're demolished is 32 years in Japan, 67 in the United States and 81 in the United Kingdom. Japanese houses get torn down around the time the mortgage is paid off. In other words, people spend their whole lives buying homes that don't last as assets. The UIA's message includes a line about reusing existing buildings. I think that's a message Japan especially needs to hear. Stop assuming we'll tear houses down after 32 years, build homes that can be used for 80, and keep repairing them so the next people can live in them. If we can do that, the next generation won't have to “buy” a home. They can “receive” one. Protecting housing as a right isn't only about continuing to build new things. How many years was your home built to last?

Background
How long Japanese homes last

According to estimates by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, the average age of demolished homes is about 32 years in Japan, about 67 in the US and about 81 in the UK, making Japan's strikingly short. Behind this are the low performance of homes built before the 1980s and a market practice in which a used house's value drops to almost zero at around 20 years old. Encouraging sales of existing homes has long been a policy goal.

The UIA and Japan

The International Union of Architects (UIA), founded in 1948, is the international body of architects' organizations, and its members from Japan include the Japan Institute of Architects (JIA). The UIA World Congress was held in Tokyo in 2011, and the congress, held every three years, also awards the UIA Gold Medal, which is ranked alongside the Pritzker Prize.

Mitsui Fudosan breaks ground on London's British Library redevelopment, a record for Japan in Europe

Mitsui Fudosan has broken ground on the British Library redevelopment in London. At about ¥230 billion, it is the largest European property investment ever made by a Japanese company. The building is designed by RSHP and is due to be completed in 2032.

Mitsui Fudosan, one of Japan's largest real estate developers, broke ground on Sept 17 on the British Library Redevelopment Project (working name) in London's King's Cross district, and on Sept 21 held a groundbreaking ceremony that included a Japanese-style jichinsai, a Shinto ritual to purify a site before construction. According to the Yomiuri Shimbun, the investment is £1.1 billion (about ¥230 billion), the largest ever European property investment by a Japanese company. Completion is expected in 2032.

The plan is to build a mixed-use building on part of the British Library's site, in steel-reinforced concrete (SRC), with 11 stories above ground and one below and a total floor area of about 96,000 m² (about 1.04 million sq ft). The library portion will add about 9,300 m² (100,000 sq ft) of new public space, connecting exhibition galleries, a learning center and event space with the existing library. The upper floors will be offices and labs, aimed at companies and research institutions in the life sciences, a field in which the UK is strong.

The design is by RSHP, known for the Pompidou Center in Paris and London's Leadenhall Building, with Arup on structural and building services engineering, Stanhope as development manager and Mace as construction manager. According to British media, the new building will also house the headquarters of the Alan Turing Institute.

Briefing
  • The project is being carried out jointly by a wholly owned subsidiary of Mitsui Fudosan UK and the British Library, and Mitsui Fudosan announced its participation in March 2025. It is the company's first lab and office development for the life sciences in the UK (Mitsui Fudosan)
  • The building will be SRC, with 11 stories above ground and one below and a total floor area of about 96,000 m² (about 1.04 million sq ft). The library portion adds about 9,300 m² (100,000 sq ft) of new public space connected to the existing building (PR TIMES)
  • According to the UK's Building Design (BD), the existing British Library, completed in the 1990s, is a Grade I listed building, the top tier of heritage protection in England. Less than a tenth of the new building's floor space will be for the library, with the rest going to offices for life sciences tenants and the new headquarters of the Alan Turing Institute

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

When people think of the British Library, many picture its red-brick walls and the statue of Newton in the plaza in front of the entrance. Completed in the 1990s, it's one of the newest generation of national buildings in the UK. Right next to it, a Japanese developer is putting up a building for ¥230 billion. And it's not just a library expansion: the upper floors are labs and offices. The library space is only about a tenth of the total, and the rest will be leased to life sciences companies. Here's the interesting part. It's a setup in which the cost of adding a public cultural facility is covered by private office rents. In Japan, too, there are more and more cases where city halls or libraries are rebuilt with hotels or offices stacked on top to make the numbers work, right? Now a Japanese company is doing that in London. The design is by RSHP, one of Britain's leading firms, and how the new building relates to the existing library will be the thing to watch. It's due to be finished in 2032. If you get the chance to go to London, step out of King's Cross Station and take a look beside the library. What will rise next to a national library over the next six years? The construction that answers that question starts now.

Background
Japanese developers abroad

Mitsui Fudosan, Mitsubishi Estate, Mori Building and others have taken on large developments in London and New York since the 2010s. It's part of a move to build up rental assets overseas with Japan's shrinking population in mind, and in London, Mitsui Fudosan is also involved in redeveloping the BBC's former television center site.

Public facilities in private projects

Building public facilities such as libraries and city halls together with private offices or housing, so that the private side helps cover the cost, is also spreading in Japan. The public side gets a new facility and the private side gets the right to develop a prime site, and whether the cultural facility keeps its quality depends on how the design and contracts are put together.
