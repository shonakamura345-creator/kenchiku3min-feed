Update: Japan starts reworking its top architect exam so university students can take it

Japan's transport and construction ministry held a subcommittee on Oct 19 to review the exam for first-class kenchikushi, the country's top architect license. With students set to be allowed to sit the exam while still enrolled, it will consider eligibility and exam content.

Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) on Oct 19 convened a subcommittee on reviewing the first-class kenchikushi exam, set up under the Central Kenchikushi Council, and laid out how it will proceed. First-class kenchikushi is Japan's top architect license, which covers buildings of any size. The review responds to the amended Kenchikushi Act, Japan's architects law, passed in July, which will let people take the exam while still at university. The subcommittee will work out changes to eligibility and exam content, hearing from experts along the way.

As we reported on Oct 8, the part of the amended law requiring written contracts for design and construction supervision takes effect on April 1, 2027. The exam-related parts, including taking the exam while enrolled, do not yet have an effective date. According to news reports, MLIT is working toward launching the new system within three years of the law's promulgation. The number of years of practical experience required to register for a first-class license is expected to stay the same.

Under the law, students will be able to take the exam on conditions such as earning the required credits at university. The Architectural Institute of Japan, the country's main academic society for architecture, says the impact on university teaching will be large. It has asked for enough time before the change takes effect, and for the views of universities that teach the designated subjects to be heard carefully and reflected in the system.

Briefing
  • The Central Kenchikushi Council is a body set up within MLIT under the Kenchikushi Act. It deliberates on matters such as pass marks for the first-class exam and disciplinary action against licensed architects. At its June 9 meeting, the future of the first-class exam was on the agenda.
  • The amended Kenchikushi Act was passed on July 24 and promulgated on July 31. On the licensing side, it also shortens the practical experience a second-class kenchikushi, a license limited to smaller buildings, needs before taking the first-class exam, from four years to two.
  • In a written opinion, the Architectural Institute of Japan said the review needs to be considered on the assumption of a move to computer-based testing (CBT).

Background
From exam to license

Passing the exam alone doesn't let you call yourself a kenchikushi. For the first-class license, university graduates register only after at least two years of practical experience. That order stays the same even once students can take the exam.

Younger test-takers

A 2020 reform changed first-class exam eligibility so that graduates can take it right after finishing university. One exam-prep school's analysis found that people in their 20s made up about 65% of those who passed this year's written exam, the first stage of the test. Taking the exam while enrolled pushes that trend a step further.

Average new condo price in greater Tokyo tops ¥100 million over a half-year for the first time

New condominiums released in the Tokyo metropolitan area from April to September averaged ¥114.94 million, the first time a half-year average has topped ¥100 million, according to the Real Estate Economic Institute.

The Real Estate Economic Institute, a private research firm that tracks Japan's housing market, said on Oct 20 that new condominiums released in the Tokyo metropolitan area (Tokyo plus the neighboring prefectures of Kanagawa, Saitama and Chiba) in the first half of fiscal 2026, April through September, averaged ¥114.94 million per unit. That was up 21.1% from a year earlier and the first time the average for the first half of a fiscal year has topped ¥100 million.

By area, the average in Tokyo's 23 wards, the city's urban core, rose 34.7% to a record ¥179.25 million. According to news reports, a string of large projects went on sale while the number of units released stayed limited, pushing up the average. Behind the rise are higher land prices on top of growing construction costs, including materials and labor.

Construction costs remain high, making the direction of prices hard to predict. As we reported on Sep 24, the August average fell for the first time in five months, but taken over six months, the upward trend continues.

Briefing
  • A year earlier, in the first half of fiscal 2025, 9,150 units were released in the Tokyo area, up 11.1% from the year before (Real Estate Economic Institute release of Oct 21, 2025).
  • Jiji Press cites rising construction costs as the reason the 23-ward average, as well as the Tokyo-area average, hit a record high.
  • TV Tokyo BIZ put soaring construction costs in its headline as a major factor behind the rise.

¥114.94 million. That's the average price per unit of new condos put on sale in the Tokyo area from April to September this year. It's the first time a half-year average has gone over ¥100 million. In Tokyo's 23 wards alone, it's ¥179.25 million. Just looking at the numbers, it sounds like a story from another country, doesn't it? What's pushing prices up is the cost of land and the cost of building. A building is the sum of the craftspeople's labor and the price of steel and concrete. Both have been creeping up over the past few years. On top of that, this time a batch of expensive large projects went on sale at the same time, which reports say lifted the average. An average is like the average height of a class, which goes up when one very tall kid transfers in. So not everyone is paying ¥100 million. Still, for now there's no sign that the cost of building will come down. As fewer people can afford a new home, who will new housing in central Tokyo end up being for?

Background
How averages behave

Average prices swing widely when a batch of units costing several hundred million yen each goes on sale at once. That's why they rise and fall from month to month. To read the trend, it's common to look at half-year or annual figures alongside the price per square meter.

Why building costs won't fall

On Japanese construction sites, an aging workforce, labor shortages and rising material prices have combined to keep pushing building costs up. The national government is also pushing pay raises for skilled workers, and higher wages feed into construction costs. The debate over paying skilled workers a monthly salary, which we reported on Oct 14, is part of the same trend.

Fortress-led group named preferred bidder for hotel at Tadao Ando's Awaji Yumebutai, plans ¥5 billion-plus

Hyogo Prefecture, which plans to sell part of Awaji Yumebutai, has chosen a group led by the Japanese arm of US-based Fortress as the preferred bidder for its hotel and international conference center. The group plans to invest more than ¥5 billion.

Hyogo Prefecture, home to Kobe, announced on Oct 14 that it had chosen a group led by Fortress Investment Group Japan, the Japanese arm of a US asset manager, as the preferred negotiating partner for the hotel, international conference center and other facilities it owns at Awaji Yumebutai, a complex in the city of Awaji. Designed by architect Tadao Ando, Awaji Yumebutai sits on Awaji Island, which lies between Kobe and the island of Shikoku. Since March, the prefecture had been seeking a buyer to take over part of the complex for a fee. Two bidders applied.

The group also includes Iconia Hospitality, which operates hotels across Japan. Its proposal positions the whole facility as Awaji Seagaia Resort and calls for an initial investment of more than ¥5 billion to add a hot spring and a kids' park, renovate guest rooms and strengthen the conference center. Renamed Awaji Seagaia Convention Center, the conference center would aim to combine meetings with stays at the resort.

The transfer covers the hotel, the observation terrace, the land and buildings of the conference center owned by the prefecture, and the hotel business. Not all of Yumebutai will become privately owned. The assets and business are to be transferred in or after April 2027, after which the new operator will take over.

Briefing
  • Fortress acquired Phoenix Seagaia Resort in Miyazaki, on the southern island of Kyushu, from Sega Sammy Holdings, and the resort is now run by Iconia Hospitality. The new Awaji resort name uses the same word, Seagaia.
  • According to Fortress's announcement, Iconia Hospitality operated 188 hotels as of October 2026.
  • The prefecture decided on a partial transfer and opened the call for bids on March 13, 2026. Companies or groups with at least five years of experience running lodging facilities in Japan were eligible.

Background
Public buildings, private owners

As running costs weigh on budgets, more local governments across Japan are selling the hotels and conference centers they built to private companies or handing over their operation. It is drawing attention as a way to keep buildings while changing who runs them, but how much they can be altered is decided by the terms of the transfer and by negotiation.

What keeps a landmark standing

Some celebrated buildings head for demolition with no new use decided, like Kenzo Tange's former Kagawa Prefectural Gymnasium, which we reported on Oct 18. Finding someone to keep using a building is itself one of the biggest conditions for saving it.
