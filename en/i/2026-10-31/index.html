Kyoto Modern Architecture Festival opens about 150 buildings across the city

The 2026 Kyoto Modern Architecture Festival opened today with about 150 buildings in 13 areas of the city. Through November 8, visitors can step inside buildings that are normally closed to the public.

The 2026 Kyoto Modern Architecture Festival, which opens up Kyoto's surviving modern buildings all at once, began on October 31 and runs for nine days through November 8. About 150 buildings are taking part across 13 areas of the city, including Kitayama and Matsugasaki in the north, the old textile district of Nishijin, Goshonishi (west of the Kyoto Imperial Palace), Higashiyama, Okazaki, and the area around Kyoto Station and Shichijo. The festival is organized by the Kyoto Modern Architecture Festival Executive Committee and the City of Kyoto.

The core of the festival is the “passport opening,” in which 61 buildings can be visited freely by anyone holding a festival passport. They open on four days split into two periods, October 31 and November 1 in the first half and November 7 and 8 in the second, and no advance booking is needed. On top of that, there are about 120 guided tours, led by experts and others, that require advance registration.

For its fifth year, the festival is also holding a special event, the Higashiyama Grand Tea Gathering. It takes its name from a Higashiyama Grand Tea Gathering held in 1921, and about 50 buildings in the Higashiyama area and neighboring districts are expected to take part.

Briefing
  • Standard passport prices, tax included, are ¥5,000 for a general pass covering the whole festival and ¥3,000 each for a pass valid only in the first or second half. The U29 full-festival pass, for visitors aged 29 and under, is kept to ¥2,000.
  • Passport openings start at 10 a.m. each day and run until 7 p.m. in the first half, including some evening openings, and until 5 p.m. in the second half. Opening days and hours differ by building, so visitors are asked to check the official website.
  • Guided tours have limited spaces and run every day of the festival. They are free for holders of a festival passport (full festival, first half, or U29); without one, the fee is ¥1,000 for general visitors, tax included.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

When people think of Kyoto, most picture temples and machiya, the city's traditional wooden townhouses, right? But Kyoto actually has plenty of modern buildings made of brick and reinforced concrete, too. Banks, schools, churches, old-style coffee shops. They're the buildings you walk right past on your way from one sightseeing spot to the next. What I like about this festival is that it lets you go inside those buildings you used to pass by. Seeing a building from the outside is like looking at the cover of a book. The curve of a stair handrail, the shape of the light that falls from a window onto the floor: you can't read those unless you go in. And some of the participating buildings are still in use as offices and schools. The whole event only works because the owners agree to open their doors. The first round of passport openings is today and tomorrow.

Background
Open-house festivals spread

Events that open many buildings at once for a set period are spreading across Japan, including Ikefes Osaka, Osaka's open-architecture festival. Ikefes Osaka 2026, which we reported on Oct 24, also drew record participation. What these events share is a chance to see offices and schools that are normally closed, thanks to the cooperation of their owners.

Opening doors to save buildings

Many modern buildings in Japan are not designated as cultural properties, and it is not unusual for owners to decide to tear them down and rebuild. When many people see the inside and learn what a building is worth, that becomes one of the things that can support the choice to keep using it.

Japan's domestic construction orders fall for first time in six years as redevelopments are rethought

Domestic construction orders tallied by the Japan Federation of Construction Contractors fell in April to September 2026, the first decline in six years. Orders from the private sector dropped 16%.

The Japan Federation of Construction Contractors (Nikkenren), the main industry group for Japan's large general contractors, released its members' domestic construction orders for April to September 2026 on October 28. It is the first decline for the first half of a fiscal year since fiscal 2020, which was hit by the coronavirus pandemic. (Japan's fiscal year starts in April.) Orders from the private sector, which make up most of the total, fell 16% from the same period a year earlier.

One reason given for the drop is that, with construction costs soaring, large redevelopment plans are being reconsidered one after another. September alone also saw domestic orders of about ¥1.9 trillion, more than 10% below the same month a year earlier.

In this app, we have reported on the scaling back of a redevelopment in central Maebashi, a city in Gunma Prefecture north of Tokyo (Oct 27), and on the delay in revising the plan to rebuild Nakano Sunplaza, a landmark complex in Tokyo's Nakano district (Oct 8). The rethinking that was happening project by project is now showing up in the numbers for the industry as a whole.

Briefing
  • Domestic orders for September alone were about ¥1.9 trillion, down just over 14% from the same month a year earlier. That means the downward trend of the first half continued into the most recent month.
  • In the first half of fiscal 2025, a year earlier, orders from the private sector grew sharply and reached their highest level in the past 20 years. This year's decline comes from that high level.
  • Nikkenren's order survey adds up the orders of its member companies, mainly large and mid-tier contractors. Its scope differs from statistics for the industry as a whole, which include small and midsize builders.

The first drop in six years. You'd think the construction industry has hit a slump, right? But actually, construction sites are still short of workers. So what's going on? The budgets of the people who want to build and the price of building no longer match. Materials and labor have both gone up, and the money set aside when a plan was drawn up can't build the same building anymore. It's close to that feeling when you open the menu at your regular restaurant and every price has been rewritten. When that happens, developers have to choose: make it smaller, push it back, or stop for now. Maebashi and Nakano were both the result of that choice. Still, I don't think smaller plans are all bad. They can be a chance to think about ways of renewing a city other than putting up a skyscraper in front of the station. So how should cities use this time while orders are down?

Background
A chain of rethinks

With construction costs rising, redevelopments around Japan are being forced to scale back or delay. In Maebashi, project costs rose 20% above the original estimate, and the plan, which included a combined elementary and junior high school, was abandoned. The revision of the plan to rebuild Nakano Sunplaza has also been pushed back to May 2027.

Orders show what's ahead

Construction orders measure the amount of work that will turn into construction over the next several years. Rather than showing how busy sites are now, they are watched as an indicator that previews the momentum of city building two to three years out.

Former Marinoa City Fukuoka site to reopen as Mitsui Outlet Park Fukuoka Marinoa in April 2027

Mitsui Fudosan and Fukuoka Jisho have named the shopping center they are building in Fukuoka's Nishi Ward. It will open in April 2027 with about 220 stores, and part of the existing buildings will be renovated and reused.

Mitsui Fudosan and Fukuoka Jisho announced on October 29 that the shopping center under construction on the site of the former Marinoa City Fukuoka, in Nishi Ward of Fukuoka City, will be called Mitsui Outlet Park Fukuoka Marinoa. Fukuoka is the largest city on Kyushu, Japan's southwestern main island. The center is scheduled to open in April 2027 with about 220 stores, the largest store count in Kyushu.

The complex will consist of two buildings: an open-air Outlet Building and a Harbor Building housing restaurants and other tenants. Under the plan, the old facility's outlet building is being replaced, while part of the existing buildings will be renovated and put back into use. Construction started in November 2025 and is due to be completed in March 2027.

Its predecessor, Marinoa City Fukuoka, opened in 2000 as Kyushu's first full-scale outlet mall. A quarter century after it opened, the site is getting a fresh start in the same place.

Briefing
  • The site covers about 85,200 m² (917,000 sq ft), and total floor area, including the parking structures, is about 117,800 m² (1.27 million sq ft). The complex consists of a new two-story commercial building, a four-story commercial building converted from an existing building, and two parking structures.
  • When construction began in November 2025, the plan called for about 200 stores and an opening in spring 2027. With this announcement, the store count has grown to about 220, and the opening has been pinned down to April 2027.
  • The Harbor Building will house a store of the supermarket chain Lopia with one of the largest sales floors in Kyushu. The announcement also named some of the planned tenants, including stores that will be opening in Kyushu for the first time.

Inside the new outlet mall, buildings from the old one will stay, renovated. This plan doesn't tear everything down and start again from an empty lot. It combines rebuilding the outlet buildings with fixing up the existing commercial building and keeping it in use. Think of a house: you rebuild the worn-out annex out back, and give the main house, which is still solid, a whole new interior. That's the picture. From a designer's point of view, starting with a completely clean slate makes for neater drawings, right? I think the reason to keep part of it anyway is that reusing a structure that still works cuts both the amount of construction and the waste from demolition. A place that was born in 2000 as Kyushu's first full-scale outlet mall is starting over with both new buildings and inherited ones. With construction costs still climbing, I think this kind of rebuild that doesn't tear everything down is going to become normal for shopping centers, too.

Background
Suburban malls reach renewal age

Large suburban shopping centers that opened across Japan around 2000 are now a quarter century old and have reached the point where owners must think about upgrading equipment or rebuilding. With construction costs high, choosing between rebuilding everything and renovating the parts that still work has become a decision that can make or break a project.
