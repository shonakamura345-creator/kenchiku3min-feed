Japan to survey whether its new standard labor costs are reaching construction workers

On Oct 5, Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) decided on a new survey of whether the labor cost standard set under the amended Construction Business Act is being followed on site. It will compare both estimates and actual wages against the standard values.

Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) decided on Oct 5 how it will survey the way the labor cost standard, known as standard labor costs, is working in practice on construction sites. The standard was set under the amended Construction Business Act, the law that governs construction contractors in Japan. The decision came at the 13th meeting of the Working Group on the Labor Cost Standard under the Central Construction Industry Council, an advisory body to the ministry. The agenda had three items: the status of measures to make the standard effective, follow-up on existing surveys, and a new survey.

According to the Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, a construction industry daily, the new survey aims to understand what is happening in private-sector building work. At the estimate stage it will look at two trades, rebar and formwork; at the wage-payment stage it will look at two others, plastering and interior finishing. Labor costs written into estimates will be compared with the standard values set for each type of work and task, and the survey will dig into individual contracting chains to check for extremely low estimates and for wages that are not reaching workers.

The standard was drawn up in December 2025 by the Central Construction Industry Council, which recommended that public and private clients and construction industry groups put it into practice. The values are set for each trade and each prefecture as a labor cost per unit of work, such as per ton or per square meter. When MLIT Minister Ibayashi met with four construction industry groups, as we reported on Sept 30, continued wage increases for skilled workers were also the top item on the agenda.

Briefing
  • The working group was set up in September 2024 and compiled a draft standard at its 11th meeting in October 2025. On Dec 2, the Central Construction Industry Council adopted the Standard for Labor Costs and recommended its implementation (MLIT)
  • The first batch of standard values covers 14 trades, and the amended Construction Business Act took full effect in December 2025. Estimates far below the standard and unjust demands from clients to cut prices were banned (Kentsu Shimbun, Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun)
  • According to Nikkei xTECH, MLIT plans to expand its surveys of how standard labor costs are being applied, with the focus on contracting chains in private-sector building work, which are harder to track than public works

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Standard labor costs are, put simply, the government's benchmark for how much a craftsperson's labor should cost for a given job. For rebar, it's this much to place one ton; for formwork, this much per square meter. The standard came in December 2025, and extremely low estimates became illegal. But having a standard is one thing. Getting the money all the way into the workers' hands is another. On a Japanese construction site, money flows down like a staircase: from the general contractor to the first-tier subcontractor, then to the second tier. If it gets shaved off somewhere along the way, the only people whose wages don't go up are the ones at the bottom actually doing the work. What makes this survey interesting is that it looks at both the estimate and the payment. It follows the labor costs written into estimates, and the wages actually paid to plasterers and interior finishers, using different trades for each. The estimate you get when you build a house has the same structure hidden inside it. An estimate below the standard may not be a bargain. It may be the result of cutting someone's wages. The survey covers four trades: rebar, formwork, plastering and interior finishing.

Background
Layers of subcontractors and wages

Japanese construction sites run on a multi-tier structure, with several layers of subcontractors under the general contractor. Each layer takes a share of the construction cost as overhead, which makes it hard for wages at the bottom tier to rise. The design labor unit prices used for public works have been raised for 14 straight years, but private-sector projects have been slow to reflect them.

Three pillars of the amended law

The amended Construction Business Act, passed in 2024, rests on three pillars: fair labor costs, passing rising material prices on to clients, and banning unreasonably short construction schedules. The labor cost standard is the core of the effort to improve workers' pay and conditions, and MLIT or prefectural governments can issue recommendations or guidance over estimates that fail to meet it.

Tokyo's Takanawa Gateway City to keep 1872 railway embankment buried, changing building plans

JR East says it will preserve two sections of the Takanawa Embankment, the remains of Japan's first railway, totaling about 160 m (525 ft), underground in Blocks 5 and 6, the second phase of Takanawa Gateway City in Tokyo, and will change its building plans to do so.

East Japan Railway Company (JR East) announced on Oct 2 that in Blocks 5 and 6 of Takanawa Gateway City, the next phase after Blocks 1 to 4, its basic policy will be to preserve part of the Takanawa Embankment underground as it is, changing its plans as far as possible. Takanawa Gateway City is the new district next to Takanawa Gateway Station on Tokyo's Yamanote Line, and Blocks 5 and 6 sit on the side closer to Shinagawa Station, a major rail hub in southern Tokyo. Two sections are covered: about 50 m (160 ft) between Blocks 5 and 6, including the remains of a signal, and about 110 m (360 ft) in the southern part of Block 6.

According to Impress Watch, on top of a change to a block road that had already been decided, JR East is also revising its building plans; in the southern part of Block 6, part of the facility plan will be changed to avoid affecting the embankment. The Takanawa Embankment is a stone-faced embankment built so that Japan's first railway, which opened between Shimbashi and Yokohama in 1872, could run over the sea. It was unearthed during the development work that began in 2019 and is valued as a site that shows the modernization of transport and the civil engineering of the time.

In Blocks 1 to 4, a section of about 80 m (260 ft) including the No. 7 bridge section and a section of about 40 m (130 ft) next to a park were added in 2021 to the national historic site “Former Shimbashi Station Site and Takanawa Embankment Site.” They are being preserved in place, with a public opening planned for fiscal 2027 (Japan's fiscal year starts in April). The signal base section is to be moved and preserved elsewhere within the blocks. The decision for Blocks 5 and 6 continues that work.

Briefing
  • Blocks 5 and 6 are the second-phase area south of Blocks 1 to 4, toward Shinagawa Station. On Oct 2, JR East published its preservation policy for the Takanawa Embankment in TAKANAWA GATEWAY CITY (Blocks 5 and 6) (JR East)
  • The sections to be preserved are about 50 m (160 ft) between Blocks 5 and 6, including the signal remains, and about 110 m (360 ft) in the southern part of Block 6. Besides changing the block road, JR East will change its building plans as far as currently possible (Impress Watch, Mainichi Shimbun)
  • The embankment remains in Blocks 1 to 4 are due to open to the public on site in fiscal 2027, and architects Hiroshi Naito and Kengo Kuma are taking part in designing the exhibition space and corridor (Nikkei xTECH)

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Takanawa Gateway City, as I also wrote on note, the Japanese blogging platform, is a district where JR East spent about ¥600 billion to line up four towers of around 160 m (525 ft). Under the ground at their feet, stonework from 150 years ago lies sleeping. This time, in the next blocks toward Shinagawa, JR East decided not to put buildings on top of those stones. The phrase to hold on to here is “preserved underground.” Instead of digging the stones out to show them, you leave them buried. On the surface it looks like empty ground, but the plans themselves have to change so that building foundations and underground piles don't damage the stonework. From the developer's point of view, it's like having two strips running under the site where nothing can be built. When the embankment was found in 2019, there was a big debate over preservation versus development. Seven years on, JR East has said that at least in Blocks 5 and 6 it will change things “as far as possible.” What do you think? How far should a ¥600 billion district go to accommodate stones that stay buried?

Background
Three ways to preserve remains

Archaeological remains can be preserved in place, left exposed and opened to the public; relocated to another spot; or reburied and left for future study, known as underground preservation. Underground preservation costs less and has less impact on development, but the remains cannot be shown. At the Takanawa Embankment, all three methods are being used, block by block.

Development and buried heritage

Under Japan's Act on Protection of Cultural Properties, when remains are found during development, the rule is to excavate and document them, while preserving them in place depends on talks with the developer. After the Takanawa Embankment was unearthed in 2020, academic societies and others called for its preservation, and JR East decided to preserve part of it in place. It has drawn attention as a large-scale preservation on privately developed land.

Foster + Partners' Agravitas extension floats a 55 m gallery over Bilbao's Fine Arts Museum

The Bilbao Fine Arts Museum in northern Spain reopened on Oct 6 after an extension designed by Foster + Partners. Large V-shaped columns hold a 55 m (180 ft) gallery up in the air above the existing buildings.

The Bilbao Fine Arts Museum in Bilbao, northern Spain, reopened on Oct 6 after four years of construction. It was designed by Foster + Partners with local architect Luis María Uriarte. The extension is named Agravitas, meaning “without gravity.” According to Dezeen, a 55 m (180 ft) bridge-like gallery is held up by large V-shaped columns and spans over the existing complex without touching its structure.

According to ArchDaily, the new steel structure increases the existing building's floor area by 60%, adding 2,300 m² (24,800 sq ft) of flexible exhibition space on two levels, along with space for education programs, workshops and offices. Lifting the new volume up frees the ground level, creating the Arriaga Atrium of about 1,000 m² (10,800 sq ft). The steel was sourced locally, a nod to Bilbao's industrial history.

The Art Newspaper reports the project cost at €45 million. Entry is free for the first week after the reopening, and the main entrance of the red-brick original building, completed in 1945, has been restored and brought back into use. Bilbao is best known for Frank Gehry's Guggenheim Museum, but this is an older institution founded in 1908, with a collection ranging from classical Spanish painting to modern and contemporary Basque art.

Briefing
  • Designed by Foster + Partners and Luis María Uriarte. Construction began in November 2022, and after about four years of work a ceremony was held on Oct 5, 2026, followed by the public opening on Oct 6. Entry is free through Oct 13 (Dezeen, local reports)
  • The extension is a bridge-like structure made of steel from Bilbao, supported on V-shaped columns without touching the existing structure. It adds 2,300 m² (24,800 sq ft) of exhibition space on two levels and a new Arriaga Atrium of about 1,000 m² (10,800 sq ft) at ground level (ArchDaily)
  • The project cost €45 million (The Art Newspaper). At the center of the atrium stands Francisco Durrio's sculptural fountain dedicated to the composer Arriaga, and the main entrance of the 1945 old building has been restored

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Floating a 55 m (180 ft) box over an existing museum. As a way to extend a building, that's pretty bold. Normally you add onto the side or the back of an old building, right? But the Bilbao Fine Arts Museum faces a park in the center of the city, and there was no land to spread out sideways. So Foster chose to build upward, and on top of that, put no load on the existing buildings. V-shaped columns stand beside the existing wings, and a steel bridge spans across them. That's why the extension looks like it's floating, and it also meant the old building didn't have to be torn down during construction. What I find interesting is that this structural choice becomes the way the architecture looks. The structural decision not to lean on the old building turned into the name “without gravity.” And the ground freed up by lifting the gallery has come back to the public as a 1,000 m² (10,800 sq ft) atrium. In Japan, too, plenty of museums and government buildings are struggling with how to extend or rebuild. Next time you see an extension to an old public building, look for the columns and find where the new part carries its weight.

Background
Extensions that leave the old alone

In extensions to historic buildings, a growing approach is to carry the new part on its own independent structure without adding load to the existing one. There is no need to closely assess the old building's seismic performance or spare foundation capacity, and the addition is easier to remove in the future, but the site needs room for the independent columns. In Japan, too, more museums and government buildings are making the same choice for their extensions.

Bilbao and architecture

Bilbao is synonymous with the “Bilbao effect,” its shift from industrial city to tourist destination after the Guggenheim Museum, designed by Frank Gehry, opened in 1997. Foster also designed the city's metro stations (1995), so this museum extension is a case of the same architect returning to the heart of the city 30 years later.
