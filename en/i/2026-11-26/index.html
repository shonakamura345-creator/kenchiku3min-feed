Meiji Yasuda Shinjuku Building, linked to Tokyo's Shinjuku Station, opens its retail floors on Nov 28

The retail area of the Meiji Yasuda Shinjuku Building, on the west side of Shinjuku Station in Tokyo, opens on Nov 28. The 23-story tower replaces a head office built in 1961, and 21 shops will fill its basement and ground floor.

Meiji Yasuda Life Insurance will open the retail area of the Meiji Yasuda Shinjuku Building, its rebuilt tower on the west side of Shinjuku Station in Tokyo's Nishi-Shinjuku 1-chome, on Nov 28. Shinjuku Station is one of the world's busiest rail hubs, and the building connects directly to it. The first basement level and the ground floor will hold 21 shops, mostly restaurants and cafes.

The building is a mixed-use office tower with 23 floors above ground and 4 below, about 126 m (410 ft) tall. It replaces the former Yasuda Life Insurance head office, completed in 1961, which had aged, and rebuilding work had been underway since 2021. The tower was completed at the end of August this year, and a completion ceremony and tour were held on Sep 4.

The retail area has 5 shops on the ground floor and 16 on the first basement level. The ground floor includes Qu'il fait bon Shinjuku, a large store of about 100 tsubo (tsubo is a traditional Japanese unit of floor area), and Shake Shack, while the basement has a conveyor-belt sushi restaurant, a bistro, a cocktail bar and more. The building also has the Meiji Yasuda Hall, and in a disaster it plans to take in people stranded when trains stop, working with Shinjuku Ward.

Briefing
  • The ground floor will have the roughly 100-tsubo Qu'il fait bon Shinjuku, plus Starbucks Tea & Cafe, the tea-focused format of Starbucks; Guzman y Gomez, a Mexican chain from Australia; the salad shop CHOPPED SALAD DAY; and the burger chain Shake Shack. In Tokyo, the Qu'il fait bon store follows its Tokyo Dome City location.
  • On the first basement level, shops include THE CITY BAKERY BISTRO RUBIN Shinjuku, a bistro run by THE CITY BAKERY; Kaiten Sushi Nemuro Hanamaru, a conveyor-belt sushi chain from Nemuro in Hokkaido; and BREW, a self-pour cocktail bar.
  • For disasters, the building has emergency generators and stockpiles for stranded commuters. The second floor has a hall and a lounge that local residents can also use.

Background
A wave of west-side rebuilds

On the west side of Shinjuku Station, large buildings in front of the station keep being rebuilt, including a skyscraper under construction on the site of the Odakyu Department Store main building. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government and Shinjuku Ward are reorganizing the area as the Shinjuku Grand Terminal, reconnecting the station and surrounding streets for people on foot. As more buildings link directly to the station, the flow of people both underground and at street level changes too.

Renewing 60-year-old buildings

Office buildings and government buildings built during Japan's high-growth era are now reaching around 60 years old and the time for rebuilding. As we reported on Nov 19 and Nov 24, the head office of Hiroshima Shinkin Bank (60 years old) and Okayama City Hall (the old building served for 58 years) are part of the same trend. Rebuilds increasingly add disaster roles that the old buildings did not have, such as sheltering stranded commuters.

Kyoto picks Terrada Warehouse group for a four-building artists' village near Kyoto Station, due in fiscal 2030

Kyoto City has chosen a group led by Terrada Warehouse to use about 4,300 m² (46,000 sq ft) of city-owned land in Higashi-Kujo, southeast of Kyoto Station. The plan is four buildings, including studios and lodging, opening in fiscal 2030.

On Nov 20, Kyoto City announced that it had chosen a group led by Terrada Warehouse (Shinagawa Ward, Tokyo), with Develop (Ichikawa, Chiba Prefecture) as joint partner, as the preferred bidder to use about 4,300 m² (46,000 sq ft) of city-owned land in Higashi-Kujo, Minami Ward. The site is in the area southeast of Kyoto Station, and the selection came from a city call for proposals aimed at building up the area around art.

The project is called Link Knot KYOTO (working name). It plans four buildings: shared studios for artists, a lodging facility, a share house and residences, with a total floor area of about 6,000 m² (65,000 sq ft). Construction is set to start in fiscal 2028 or later, with opening targeted for fiscal 2030. (Japan's fiscal year runs from April to March.)

The land will be leased for 60 years, and the proposed rent is ¥33.6 million a year. That equals the minimum set in the call for proposals, and this group was the only applicant. The contract with the city is expected to be signed in fiscal 2027 or later.

Briefing
  • The site is city-owned land in Higashi-Kujo Kitakawara-cho, Minami Ward, covering about 4,352 m² (46,800 sq ft). Kyoto City opened the call for proposals in May 2026 and reviewed the proposals at a closed selection committee meeting on Oct 27.
  • The project cost and the income and expense plan for each use have not been made public.
  • Kyoto City is putting its land in this area to use step by step. In April 2025, for a different city-owned plot (about 1,573 m² (16,900 sq ft)), it chose Kaikai Kiki, the company of artist Takashi Murakami, as the preferred bidder, and the company plans to build a production studio there.

Studios, a lodging facility, a share house, residences. That's the lineup of the four buildings going up southeast of Kyoto Station. The ones who will see that through are probably the people who live around here.

Background
Turning the station's southeast into an art district

In the area southeast of Kyoto Station, the city has been leasing its land to private companies to turn it into bases for art and creative work. In 2021, teamLab was chosen as the preferred bidder for another city-owned plot, and in 2025 the Kaikai Kiki studio plan was decided. Kyoto City University of Arts moved nearby in 2023, and arts facilities are gathering on the southeast side of the station.

Leasing instead of selling

Across Japan, more local governments are leasing their land for decades instead of selling it, and letting private companies build on it. The land stays a public asset, and rent comes in every year. On the other hand, it becomes important to decide in the first contract what happens to the buildings at the end of the term, and what to do if the business does not go well.

London Museum opens its new home in a restored 1880s Smithfield market building on Nov 28, a £437 million project

London Museum will open its new home in the Victorian General Market at Smithfield, central London, on Nov 28. The central dome has been restored, and the permanent galleries are free.

London Museum, formerly the Museum of London, will open its new home on Nov 28 in the General Market at Smithfield in central London, a converted market building. The Victorian market hall was built in the 1880s and had long stood unused.

The design was led by Stanton Williams, with Asif Khan Studio as collaborating architect and Julian Harrap Architects in charge of conservation. The firms were chosen in a 2016 design competition, and the project took about ten years. The total cost is put at £437 million. The dome at the center of the market has also been restored.

The permanent galleries are made up of three connected spaces called Real Time, Our Time and Past Time, and admission is free. The neighboring Poultry Market, from the 1960s, is set to open in 2028 with temporary exhibition galleries, collection stores and a learning center.

Briefing
  • The design team was picked in a 2016 competition that included leading firms such as BIG and Caruso St John.
  • The first season after opening is London Tastes, running until Aug 31, 2027. Free admission tickets can be booked for dates from opening day through Dec 31.
  • Opening hours are 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Sunday through Thursday, with the museum staying open until 11 p.m. on Fridays and Saturdays.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

London's old Smithfield market is a market building put up in the 1880s. Now it's being reborn as a museum about the history of London. Here's the thing: market buildings actually suit museums. They were built for carts and crowds coming in and out, so the ceilings are high and the columns are far apart. Like a school gym, it's easy to change how the inside is used later on. The team led by Stanton Williams also included an office that specializes in conservation. The big dome in the middle has been restored, too. Until now, the museum was in the Barbican area of the city. From there, it's moving into a market from the 1880s. Debates over whether to keep old buildings come up in Japan all the time, don't they? Change what a building is used for, and it can go back to work. I think that's the answer London took ten years to build.

Background
Converting market halls

Nineteenth-century markets, stations and warehouses built of iron and glass have often been converted into museums or shops because of their large open spaces. The Musee d'Orsay in Paris reuses a train station from 1900, and Tate Modern in London is a converted power station. This project follows in that line.

A contrast with Japan

In Japan, as with the Osaka Shochikuza theater we reported on Oct 27, even buildings around 100 years old sometimes head to demolition before any plan for the site is decided. In London Museum's case, an unused market building was given a new role as a museum and kept as a public facility.
