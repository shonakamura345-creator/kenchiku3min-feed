Taisei and Kajima suspended for 120 days over maglev bid-rigging, delaying Shizuoka tunnel to March or later

On Oct 23, Japan's transport ministry ordered Taisei and Kajima, convicted in the Chuo Shinkansen maglev bid-rigging case, to suspend business for 120 days. JR Central says work on the Shizuoka section will not start until March next year at the earliest.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) on Oct 23 ordered Taisei Corporation and Kajima Corporation to suspend business under the Construction Business Act. Both companies' convictions in a bid-rigging case over construction of the Chuo Shinkansen, the maglev line that Central Japan Railway Company (JR Central) is building between Tokyo and Nagoya, have been finalized. The suspension runs for 120 days, from November 7 to March 6, 2027, and covers private-sector civil engineering work. During that period, the two companies cannot sign new contracts, submit estimates or negotiate deals.

In the case, the companies were charged under the Antimonopoly Act with deciding in advance which company would win the contracts for work at Shinagawa Station in Tokyo and at Nagoya Station. In September, the Supreme Court rejected both companies' appeals, finalizing their convictions.

The section hit hardest is the one in Shizuoka Prefecture, where construction has yet to begin. A joint venture that includes Taisei had been negotiating a construction contract with JR Central. After the suspension order, JR Central said it was “now effectively unable to move the negotiations forward,” and said tunnel excavation would begin no earlier than March next year. It plans to resume talks promptly once the suspension ends.

Briefing
  • The order covers private-sector projects in the civil engineering category of the companies' construction licenses and takes effect on November 7. Both companies have said they will strengthen their compliance systems.
  • The Shizuoka section is about 8.9 km (5.5 miles) long. JR Central says the suspension will delay the start of work, but it is sticking to its plan to announce an opening date for the line within this year.
  • According to media reports, the order makes it difficult to start tunnel excavation this year, and construction is expected to slip to next spring or later. JR Central called the finding that the two companies violated the Antimonopoly Act “extremely regrettable.”

120 days. The company sign stays up, but for that whole stretch the firm can't discuss any new private civil engineering work at all. If it were a shop, I'd say it's like being allowed to stay open but not being allowed to take any new reservations for four months. Where this really bites is the tunnel in Shizuoka. After long talks with the prefecture, negotiations toward a construction contract had finally started moving. And the other party at the table was the joint venture that includes Taisei. In other words, the schedule is stopping not because of anything about the work itself, but because of the contractor's past rule-breaking. The bigger the project, the fewer major firms you can trust it to, so when one company stumbles, the whole project has no choice but to wait. Bid-rigging, known in Japan as dango, has long been tied to the industry's old habit of taking turns handing out work. Now the bill for that is landing on the construction schedule. The suspension ends on March 6, 2027.

Background
How Shizuoka got stuck

The Shizuoka section of the maglev line was held up for a long time by talks with the prefecture over the project's effects on the water resources of the Oi River and the environment of the Southern Alps. It was the only section between Shinagawa and Nagoya where construction had not started. Just as those talks began to move forward, the project has been held up again, this time by problems on the contractor's side.

What a suspension means

A business suspension under the Construction Business Act is one of the heaviest administrative penalties a Japanese construction company can face. Work already under contract can continue, but the company cannot pursue new orders. Even for major firms, the impact on business and order plans is significant.

A record 224 buildings open their doors in Osaka as Ikefes Osaka 2026 begins its main weekend

Ikefes Osaka 2026, which opens buildings across Osaka to the public at the same time, entered its main period on Oct 24 and 25. A record 224 buildings are taking part in this year's festival, the 13th.

The Living Architecture Museum Festival Osaka 2026, known as Ikefes Osaka, which opens buildings across the city to the public free of charge, reaches its main period today, October 24, running through the 25th. A record 224 buildings and other sites are set to take part in this year's festival, the 13th edition.

The festival is split into a pre-period from the 20th to the 23rd, the main period on the 24th and 25th, and an after-period from the 26th to the 30th. Opening hours, capacity and reservation requirements differ from building to building, and some guided tours require entering a lottery in advance.

Osaka City Hall's main building is taking part again after joining last year. On the 24th and 25th, it is specially opening the second-floor gallery of its front entrance hall, which is normally closed to the public, and offering guided tours of staff-only rooms and the roof. Harada Sangyo is also opening its Osaka headquarters, completed in 1928, which is normally closed to the public because it is still used as offices.

Briefing
  • The second-floor gallery at City Hall's main building is open from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. on the 24th and from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. on the 25th. On the 25th, there is also a tour where visitors can ring the Miotsukushi Bell, which normally cannot be rung (advance application required, chosen by lottery).
  • From October 20 to 30, the first-floor hall of City Hall's main building is hosting exhibits and workshops about Osaka's streets and architecture. No reservation is needed, except for the workshops and some exhibits.
  • City Hall's main building joined Ikefes for the first time in 2025, so this is its second year. The list of participating buildings and the booking status of each program can be checked on the official website and in the official guidebook.

Have you ever been inside the buildings along a street you walk down every day? You see them from outside, but you have no idea what's past the entrance. Cities are full of buildings like that. Ikefes Osaka is a festival that throws those doors open all at once, for just two days. This year there are 224 of them: bank head offices, long-established company buildings, even City Hall. One way to enjoy a building is to look at its ceiling height. Older office buildings often have much higher ceilings than today's. In the days before air conditioning, that was partly a way to hold a large volume of air and get through the summer heat. Harada Sangyo's Osaka headquarters was completed in 1928, and its staff still work there every day. You're not touring a preserved building; you're dropping in on one that's still in use. I think that's what sets it apart from other open-house events. If you're walking around Osaka this weekend, step through the front door and look straight up first.

Background
Open-house events

Open House London is the best-known event that opens buildings to the public all at once, and the idea has spread around the world. In Japan, besides Ikefes Osaka, similar events are held in Tokyo, Kyoto and other cities.

Preserving by using

There are two ways to keep an old building: stop using it and preserve it like a museum, or keep it in use as a working building. Most of the buildings in Ikefes take the second route, and opening them to the public is only possible with their owners' cooperation.

Update: Japan's expert panel on condo repair bid-rigging holds first meeting to back owners' associations

On Oct 19, Japan's transport ministry held the first meeting of an expert panel set up after bid-rigging on large-scale condominium repairs. It will discuss how to help owners' associations choose contractors and compile measures by the end of the fiscal year.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) held the first meeting of an expert panel on Oct 19 to prevent a repeat of bid-rigging on large-scale repair work for condominium buildings. This follows up on our Oct 6 report on the Japan Fair Trade Commission (JFTC) finding of bid-rigging and the ministry's plan to set up a review panel.

In September, the JFTC, Japan's antitrust watchdog, found bid-rigging on a total of 171 large-scale repair projects in Tokyo and six other prefectures in the surrounding Kanto region, and determined that two design consultants and 40 contractors had violated the Antimonopoly Act through unreasonable restraint of trade. The panel will discuss ways to help owners' associations, which are made up of residents, choose appropriate firms when they order repair work. The ministry plans to compile measures by the end of the fiscal year, which in Japan ends in March.

The meeting was closed to the public except for its opening. According to the ministry, panel members said owners' associations lack the information they need to decide how to choose contractors and whether estimates are reasonable. The ministry also noted that associations at condominiums found to have had work done under bid-rigging have been asking for advice about seeking damages.

Briefing
  • On Sept 29, MLIT asked industry groups to take steps to prevent a recurrence, and at the time announced its plan to set up an expert panel.
  • At the start of the first meeting, the head of MLIT's Housing Bureau said bid-rigging that betrays the trust of owners' associations cannot be tolerated. The meeting also shared information on how large-scale repairs are actually carried out and on owners' associations' lack of knowledge and know-how.
  • The panel will look at what owners' associations should watch out for and at ways to secure appropriate contractors. After compiling its findings at the end of the fiscal year, it is expected to keep studying the issue, including steps for fiscal 2027 and beyond.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Large-scale repairs on a condominium building come around roughly once every 12 to 15 years. It's the job where scaffolding goes up and the whole building gets wrapped in white sheeting. The money comes from the repair reserve fund that residents have been paying into every month. And the one placing the order is the owners' association, in other words, representatives of the residents. But most residents aren't construction experts, right? Even if three companies hand in estimates, they can't tell which one is fair. That's why they turn to a design consultant for advice. This time, though, even that adviser was found to have joined in the bid-rigging. Imagine you're selling your home, and the real estate agent you asked for an appraisal turns out to be secretly working with the buyer. It's a setup a lot like that. When panel members said residents lack the information to judge, I think that's exactly the point. How do you give residents the ability to choose contractors? Or do you build a system that doesn't leave it all up to them? Where do you think that line should be drawn?

Background
The consultant's role

In large-scale repairs, a common approach is the design-and-supervision method, in which an owners' association hires a design consultant to survey the building, draw up the work specifications and help choose a contractor. The consultant is supposed to be on the residents' side, checking construction costs, which is why its involvement in bid-rigging makes the problem so serious.

Reserve funds running short

Rising material and labor costs keep pushing up the price of repair work, and many condominiums don't have enough in their repair reserve funds. MLIT has also begun revising its guidelines for long-term repair plans. Both the money for repairs and the way the work is ordered are now being called into question.
