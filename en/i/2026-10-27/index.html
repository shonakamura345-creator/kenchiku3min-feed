Maebashi scales back downtown redevelopment as its ¥47 billion cost climbs, dropping a planned school

The preparatory association behind a redevelopment in Maebashi's Chiyoda-machi district has announced a major overhaul. With construction costs soaring, it will drop a combined elementary and junior high school and build the library as a standalone building.

On Oct 23, the redevelopment preparatory association formed by the city and landowners announced that it will substantially revise and scale back a redevelopment in Chiyoda-machi, in central Maebashi, the capital of Gunma Prefecture north of Tokyo. The total project cost had been put at ¥47 billion, but rising material and labor costs are expected to push it up by about 20%, and the association concluded that the plan would be hard to carry out as it stood.

The revised proposal scraps a complex that would have combined an education and culture facility, housing a combined elementary and junior high school, with the city library and other uses. The library will instead be built as a standalone building. The proposal also calls for renovating, rather than demolishing, the newer annex of the Suzuran department store's Maebashi branch, which is set to close, and using it for retail and other purposes to hold down costs. Whether to include offices or condominiums is still under consideration.

At a press conference on Oct 26, Maebashi Mayor Akira Ogawa described the revision as a step "to move forward" and said the city intends to push ahead with the project in cooperation with the private sector. Locally, people have voiced both hope that the plan will finally get moving and worry about what losing the school will mean for the life of the town center.

Briefing
  • The revision covers the redevelopment planned for the central hub district of Chiyoda-machi. The preparatory association was set up in October 2018, and the city and landowners have been drawing up the plan together.
  • Maebashi City left funding for this downtown redevelopment out of its initial budget proposal for fiscal 2026. The establishment of the association has also fallen behind the original timeline, and the revised proposal is meant as a reset to make up for those delays.
  • The Suzuran Maebashi store, which stands within the redevelopment area, has said it will not be a tenant in the facilities built after the redevelopment. Under the plan to renovate and reuse the annex, the next challenge will be deciding what shops and facilities will fill the space the department store leaves behind.

Background
Revisions across Japan

NHK, Japan's public broadcaster, reported in May 2026 that about 70% of redevelopment projects around train stations nationwide had been forced to revise their plans or had been canceled or suspended. Soaring construction costs weigh most heavily on regional cities, where it is harder to recover building costs through tenant rents.

Redevelopment without demolition

Redevelopment in Japan has typically meant clearing away old buildings and putting up high-rises. But with construction costs soaring, renovating and keeping existing buildings has become an option in many places. The whole-building renovation of a condominium in Kobe's Higashinada Ward, which we reported on Oct 18, is part of the same trend of reusing a structural frame to keep costs down.

Hakone Museum of Art pre-opens Oct 30 after first full renovation since 1952, walls in original color

The Hakone Museum of Art, closed since May, reopens on Oct 30. In its first full renovation since opening in 1952, its exterior walls have been returned to the pale yellow they had when the museum first opened.

The Hakone Museum of Art in Hakone, a hot-spring resort town in the mountains of Kanagawa Prefecture southwest of Tokyo, will hold a pre-opening on Oct 30 after completing renovation work. The museum had been fully closed for the work since May 7, 2026, and its grand opening is scheduled for Dec 23. It is the museum's first full-scale renovation since it opened in 1952, covering the buildings, galleries, gardens and rest areas all at once.

On the outside, the exterior walls of the main building and the annex are being returned to the pale yellow they had at the opening, a traditional Japanese color known as torinoko-iro. The aim is to bring the buildings closer to their original look, paired with their blue tile roofs. In the main building's galleries, new display cases have been made by Kenji Suda, a woodworker designated a Living National Treasure, a title Japan gives to master artisans and performers who carry on traditional crafts and arts. The cases follow the founder's designs and use sashimono, a traditional technique of joining pieces of wood.

A new cafe has been added to the rest area, with a counter made of prized wood by Akira Murayama, another woodworker who is a Living National Treasure. During the pre-opening period, visitors can see Shinsenkyo, the museum's garden, which is designated a Place of Scenic Beauty and known for its moss garden and its stone garden, Sekirakuen, along with an inner garden that is normally closed to the public.

Briefing
  • Admission during the pre-opening period is listed as ¥1,000 for adults, ¥500 for high school and university students, and free for junior high school students and younger. After the grand opening, adult admission is expected to be ¥1,500.
  • The moss garden at Shinsenkyo is said to have about 130 kinds of moss and about 200 maple trees. At the new cafe, visitors will be able to enjoy specialty coffee and original breads while looking out over the moss garden.
  • The museum's founder was Mokichi Okada. The original three-story reinforced concrete building, its interiors and its display cases are all said to have been designed by Okada.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Torinoko-iro is a white with a faint yellow tint, like the shell of a hen's egg. When you hear "renovation," you'd assume it means repainting in a fresh, clean new color, right? This time it's the opposite: they turned the clock back to the day the museum opened. The color of the exterior walls is the single biggest factor in how a building comes across. The same house looks completely different in the evening light depending on whether its walls are pure white or egg-colored. What's more, the Hakone Museum of Art is a museum meant to be seen together with its garden. The wall color, set between the green of the moss and the blue of the roof tiles, is practically part of the garden. Even the display cases were remade by Living National Treasures, which I think shows an approach that treats the building and what's inside it as one. The first day of the pre-opening is Oct 30.

Background
Restoring the original color

In renovating historic buildings in Japan, the idea of restoration, cleaning off grime and bringing back the original materials and colors, has been spreading. As in the preservation and restoration of the red-brick Marunouchi building of Tokyo Station, the usual process now is to confirm the original specifications through research before restoring them.

Garden and building as one

Many Japanese museums make the garden or the surrounding scenery, rather than the building itself, the main attraction. At such places, a key design condition even in a renovation is to keep the building from standing out too much and to protect the views of the garden.

Osaka Shochiku-za, the 103-year-old 'Arc de Triomphe of Dotonbori', to be demolished from November

Demolition of the Osaka Shochiku-za theater in Osaka's Dotonbori district, which closed in May, will begin in November. The work is expected to take about two years, and no concrete plan for the site has been decided.

Demolition of the Osaka Shochiku-za, a theater in Dotonbori, Osaka's canal-side entertainment district, will begin in November, it emerged on Oct 23 from inquiries to Shochiku, the company that runs it. The work is expected to take about two years. The theater closed after its May 2026 performances, ending a 103-year history.

The Osaka Shochiku-za was built in 1923, designed and constructed by Obayashi Corporation. Tokusaburo Kimura, the Obayashi engineer who led the design, is said to have modeled it on La Scala in Milan, Italy. Its Neo-Renaissance facade, marked by a large arch at the front, came to be called "the Arc de Triomphe of Dotonbori" and was long cherished as the face of the neighborhood.

The building underwent a major renovation in 1997 that kept the front facade while turning the interior into a theater with up-to-date equipment. Shochiku had said it aims to make the site "a new hub for culture and the performing arts," but says no concrete plan for after the demolition has been decided.

Briefing
  • The theater closed on May 26, 2026, and the final performance of the run that day was its last. After the closure was announced, Shochiku was reported to be in talks with Osaka Prefecture and Osaka City, with rebuilding as one of the options.
  • According to Obayashi Corporation's company history, the Osaka Shochiku-za was completed in January 1923. It is said to have been built as Japan's first steel-reinforced concrete theater for showing Western films.
  • The demolition is expected to take about two years, meaning large-scale construction will continue in the heart of Dotonbori. The future use of the site, the size of any new building and when construction would start have all yet to be decided.

"The Arc de Triomphe of Dotonbori." That's what people have called the big arch on the front of the Osaka Shochiku-za. Here's the thing: this building already had almost everything inside it replaced once, in 1997. They kept only the front wall and built a brand-new theater behind it. Think of it as putting a new painting into an old frame; that makes it easy to picture, doesn't it? Keeping just the face of a building like this is called facade preservation, and it has been widely used in Japan as a way to keep using buildings while protecting the look of the street. This time, the frame itself is going too. Because there's no plan yet for the site, whether the memory of that arch will be carried into the next building is also a blank page. How do you pass on 103 years of a neighborhood's face? I think that's where the real homework begins.

Background
Keeping only the face

Facade preservation, in which only the outer walls are kept and the interior is rebuilt, has been used in many office redevelopments, including in Tokyo's Marunouchi business district and Osaka's Nakanoshima area. It keeps costs down while protecting the streetscape, but it has also drawn criticism for losing the building's interior, and opinion on it as a preservation method is divided.

Why theaters are hard to rebuild

Large theaters need a deep stage and a tall auditorium, so rebuilding one takes a large site and a lot of money. With construction costs soaring, some closed theaters' sites have been turned to other uses, and whether a theater can be rebuilt as a theater has become an issue in many places.
