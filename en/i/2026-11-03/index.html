Tokyo's imperial art museum Sannomaru Shozokan fully opens with eight times the gallery space

Sannomaru Shozokan, the museum of the Japanese imperial family's art collection in the East Gardens of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo, fully opened on November 3 after a two-phase rebuild. It now has three galleries and about eight times the exhibition space it had before.

The Sannomaru Shozokan (the Museum of the Imperial Collections), which holds art passed down through Japan's imperial family, fully opened on November 3, Culture Day, a national holiday. The museum is in Tokyo's Chiyoda Ward, inside the East Gardens of the Imperial Palace, the part of the palace grounds in central Tokyo that is open to the public. The press was shown inside the day before, on November 2.

The project replaced the old museum in two phases. The Phase 1 building opened on November 3, 2023, when the museum took its current name, and the Phase 2 building, built on the site of the old museum, has now been added.

With the full opening, the museum has three galleries, and its exhibition space has grown about eightfold, from about 160 m² (1,700 sq ft) in the old facility to about 1,300 m² (14,000 sq ft). Management has moved from the Imperial Household Agency, the government body that handles the affairs of the imperial family, to the National Institutes for Cultural Heritage, which also runs the Tokyo and Kyoto National Museums. Next door, the Ote rest house opened at the same time, bringing the first cafe ever to the Imperial Palace.

A special exhibition on the treasures of the Imperial Palace marks the opening and runs until January 24, 2027. It brings together all 30 scrolls of Colorful Realm of Living Beings, the celebrated set of bird-and-flower paintings by the eighteenth-century Kyoto painter Ito Jakuchu. It is also the first time treasures from the Shosoin, the eighth-century imperial storehouse in Nara, have been shown to the public at the Imperial Palace.

Briefing
  • The design is by Nikken Sekkei. The Imperial Household Agency commissioned the firm in 2016 for design work on the extension and renovation of the Sannomaru Shozokan, so the same architect saw the two-phase rebuild through from start to finish.
  • The Phase 1 building opened on November 3, 2023, and at that point the museum's Japanese name gained the prefix Kokyo, meaning Imperial Palace. The full opening falls exactly three years later, on the same Culture Day.
  • The special exhibition also includes the first public showing of Shosoin treasures at the Imperial Palace.

From 160 m² (1,700 sq ft) to about 1,300 m² (14,000 sq ft). The old galleries were about the size of a slightly roomy convenience store, the kind of konbini you find on every corner in Japan. The imperial family's collection includes thousands of masterpieces, yet only a tiny handful could be shown at any one time. This project widened that window by rebuilding the museum in two rounds. Here's the thing: splitting it in two was the key. First you build Phase 1 so the collection has somewhere to move. Then you tear down the emptied old building and put Phase 2 on its site. Finding a warehouse to store artworks is hard in itself, so the whole thing ends up like renovating a shop while keeping it open, right? The location adds a condition no other museum faces: it's inside the Imperial Palace. In the East Gardens, surrounded by stone walls and moats, the new building had to sit quietly without standing out too much. Jakuchu's 30 scrolls can hang together in one room only because of this extra space. The special exhibition runs until January 24, 2027.

Background
Rebuilding while staying open

To rebuild a museum while it still holds its collection, you first build new storage, move everything in, and then demolish the emptied building. Here, about three years passed between the completion of the Phase 1 building and the full opening. This kind of phased rebuild is common when updating buildings that can't simply shut down, such as museums and hospitals.

From palace agency to national body

The Sannomaru Shozokan was originally a facility of the Imperial Household Agency, but its management moved to the National Institutes for Cultural Heritage along with the rebuild. It marks a shift to a setup that presents the imperial family's art to the public as national cultural property.

Japan's contractors and developers split over how construction sites should take days off

Nikkenren, the lobby group for Japan's general contractors, and Fudokyo, the association of major real estate developers, have disclosed where their talks on securing construction workers stand. They are moving closer on sharing training costs but disagree on how sites should take days off.

The Japan Federation of Construction Contractors (Nikkenren), the main lobby group for Japan's general contractors, and the Real Estate Companies Association of Japan (Fudokyo), whose members include the country's largest developers, disclosed in late October where their talks on securing the construction workforce stand. This year the two groups set up their first-ever forum spanning the two industries, and a working-level steering committee has been discussing issues such as construction schedules and working conditions.

In the talks, there were positive views on considering a system in which the real estate industry, as the client that commissions the buildings, also shares the cost of training skilled tradespeople and other workers. On securing days off at construction sites, however, the two sides remain far apart. Fudokyo proposed a shift system, in which workers take turns having days off, to keep schedules from stretching out. Nikkenren, citing the difficulty of finding replacement workers and the burden of handovers, took the position that the basic approach should be closing entire sites on Saturdays and Sundays.

The two groups plan to draw up a policy by around spring 2027. Member companies would follow it on a voluntary basis, and the groups also expect to make joint requests to the government.

Briefing
  • In January 2024, ahead of the so-called 2024 problem, when caps on overtime work were set to start applying to the construction industry, Nikkenren had already asked Fudokyo to cooperate on work-style reform, including setting reasonable construction schedules. The new forum goes a step further, bringing clients and contractors to the same table to discuss these issues.
  • Besides securing workers, the forum covers several topics, including rising construction costs and maintaining the industry's capacity to build. The steering committee plans to meet several more times during fiscal 2026 (Japan's fiscal year runs April to March) on themes such as recruiting foreign workers.
  • The policy will not be legally binding; companies are expected to follow it voluntarily. The next question is how far the clients' share of costs will actually be reflected in real contracts.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Even under the same goal of two days off a week, there are two ways to take them. You either lock the gates of the whole site on weekends, or you keep the site running every day and rotate the people. From a developer's point of view, the shift system keeps schedules from slipping. If a building is finished a month late, tenant move-ins and rental income get pushed back by that much. But the contractors have their reasons too. Construction work isn't like running the register at a convenience store, where anyone who steps in does the same job. Handing over, every single day, how far the rebar got yesterday and which tradesperson was asked to do what is harder than you'd imagine, isn't it? One more important point is the workers' side. If the site is closed on weekends, they can line up their days off with their families. I think that's what will count in making this a job young people choose. The client sets the schedule and puts up the money; the builder gathers the people and runs the site. So which of them should decide what days off look like?

Background
The client's turn to change

Two days off a week at construction sites has spread so far mainly through public works commissioned by national and local governments, which build the days off into schedules and budgets. In private projects, the client's understanding is essential, so the stance of the big developers, the major clients for condominiums and office buildings, has been key.

Site closures vs. days off

Japan's construction industry distinguishes between two days off a week on a rotating basis, where each worker takes two days off weekly, and closing sites eight days every four weeks, where the site itself shuts two days a week. Closures make days off more certain but tend to lengthen schedules, and which one becomes the norm is where the interests of clients and contractors split.

Takenaka Corporation launches a space architecture office to develop lunar infrastructure

Takenaka Corporation, one of Japan's five largest general contractors, set up a Space Architecture Promotion Office in its technology division on November 1. It will develop designs for living spaces in space and construction technology that works without people on site.

Takenaka Corporation, one of Japan's five largest general contractors, established a Space Architecture Promotion Office within its technology division on November 1. The company describes it as a full-fledged setup for continuing to invest management resources in the space architecture business.

The office will develop technology for designing living spaces in space, for autonomous construction, in which machines carry out the work without people present at the site, and for building infrastructure on the Moon. It also plans to work more closely with JAXA (the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency) and with organizations overseas.

Until now, this work had been handled by the Takenaka Space Architecture Task Force, a volunteer group of employees that developed related technology and built partnerships with industry, academia and government. The new office builds on that activity and becomes the unit that takes corporate responsibility for the space architecture business.

Briefing
  • Since fiscal 2024, the company has taken part in research and development on a lunar base camp jointly led by the University of Tokyo, Kyushu University and JAXA. It has also worked on research into the comfort of living spaces, such as growing and eating vegetables in space.
  • The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) is working with the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) on a program to develop innovative technology for unmanned construction in space, with construction on the Moon in mind. One of its aims is to apply unmanned construction technology to building sites on Earth as well.
  • The new office was announced as part of an organizational restructuring, effective November 1, that Takenaka disclosed in October. The office will keep working with the task force that preceded it.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

On the Moon, there are no tradespeople and no heavy-equipment operators. It's about 380,000 km (236,000 miles) from Earth, and just getting materials there is a huge job. That's why technology that lets things get built without people sits at the center of space architecture. Think of it as asking a robot vacuum to build your house. Machines take instructions, then level the ground and stack blocks on their own. Here's the interesting part: this technology comes straight back to construction sites on Earth. We're short on tradespeople, and we want less work at night and in dangerous places. Autonomous construction honed in space should help with exactly those problems here on the ground. The other part is quality of life. In a cramped living space in space, how do you place the windows, and how do you let people feel light and greenery? That's very similar to the questions you wrestle with when designing a house. A volunteer group becomes an official department of the company. I think space architecture has entered that stage.

Background
General contractors in space

Among Japan's major general contractors, research aimed at building and living on the Moon is gradually spreading. Many companies take part in the national program for unmanned construction technology, and remote-control and automation technology is first being put to use at construction sites on Earth to deal with labor shortages.
