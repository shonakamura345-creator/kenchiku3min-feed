Kawasaki city official arrested on suspicion of leaking confidential bid prices for painting and waterproofing work

Kanagawa Prefectural Police arrested a senior official of Kawasaki, a city between Tokyo and Yokohama, on November 4 on suspicion of leaking undisclosed construction prices to an industry group ahead of bids for city painting and waterproofing jobs.

On November 4, Kanagawa Prefectural Police arrested a senior manager (59) in the urban development bureau of Kawasaki, a large city between Tokyo and Yokohama, on suspicion of violating the Act on Elimination and Prevention of Involvement in Bid Rigging (obstruction of bidding), a law aimed at public officials who help rig public tenders. He is suspected of leaking the city's undisclosed "construction price" to contractors ahead of tenders for painting and waterproofing work ordered by the city. Police have not said whether he admits the allegations.

According to the allegations, around 2024, in open competitive tenders for work at two city facilities, he passed the construction prices to the secretary-general of the Kawasaki Painting Contractors Association, an industry group, and conspired so that two member companies would win the contracts. At the time, he was head of the bureau's facilities department. The secretary-general is a former senior city official. Police believe the secretary-general used the prices he obtained to work out the minimum acceptable bid, and that this figure was then passed on to the contractors.

Police searched the association's office and Kawasaki City Hall. Suspecting that similar wrongdoing, also involving former city employees, had been repeated, they are questioning people involved to establish the full picture.

Briefing
  • According to reports, the jobs in question were painting and waterproofing work at two city facilities, both put out to open competitive tender. The construction price is the city's estimate, calculated from unit costs, and is not made public before the tender.
  • The secretary-general is reported to be a former senior official who retired from the city at the mandatory age in 2009. Police believe someone in the association acted as a coordinator who decided which company would win.
  • Also on November 4, it was reported that the Shizuoka District Public Prosecutors Office had indicted three people, including a Shizuoka prefectural employee and an executive of a civil engineering company, who had been arrested over work ordered by the prefecture, on charges including violating the same anti-bid-rigging law.

The minimum acceptable bid is the line in a public tender that says, "bid below this and you're disqualified." If a job is won at a price that's too low, cutting corners and squeezing subcontractors become more likely. The system exists to prevent that. If the price of a facility built with your tax money was being decided this way, you couldn't stay calm about it, right? In this case, at the center of the allegations is an industry group whose secretary-general is a former senior city official. Background
Why prices are kept secret

Many local governments in Japan decide for themselves whether to publish the ceiling price or the minimum acceptable bid before a tender. Publishing them in advance reduces attempts to find out the price, but critics point out that bids then cluster at the published figure and the tender turns into a lottery. Keeping them secret means officials who know the price can become targets instead, so both approaches have weak points.

Rigging in private work too

In October, Japan's Fair Trade Commission found that 42 companies had rigged bids for large-scale repair work on condominiums, and the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) began looking at ways to make ordering fairer. In both public and private work, how clients develop the ability to judge prices has become a shared challenge for the construction industry.

LaLaport TOKYO-BAY near Tokyo starts phase two of its north wing rebuild, with a full reopening set for summer 2028

Construction has begun on the second phase of the north wing rebuild at LaLaport TOKYO-BAY, a shopping mall in Funabashi just east of Tokyo. It opens in summer 2028, bringing the whole mall to about 600 stores.

Mitsui Fudosan announced on November 4 that it has started construction on the phase two area of its north wing rebuild at Mitsui Shopping Park LaLaport TOKYO-BAY in Funabashi, Chiba Prefecture, a suburb just east of Tokyo. The opening is planned for summer 2028, which will complete the rebuild and mark the mall's full reopening.

LaLaport TOKYO-BAY opened in 1981 as the first LaLaport, now a large chain of shopping malls. The north wing is being rebuilt in two phases, and the phase one area opened with 96 stores in October 2025. The phase two area will grow from about 150 stores before the rebuild to about 200, bringing the whole mall to about 600 stores.

In phase two, the former central plaza will be expanded to about three times its size, creating an indoor "stadium court" that can host sports and entertainment events.

Briefing
  • The north wing's total store area is planned to grow from about 45,000 m² (484,000 sq ft) before the rebuild to about 60,200 m² (648,000 sq ft).
  • No specific date has been given for the full reopening yet; it is planned for "summer 2028."

Background
Malls reach rebuilding age

Many large suburban shopping centers that opened in Japan in the 1980s and 1990s have reached the point where their buildings and equipment need renewal. Across the country, owners are split between closing and demolishing, or rebuilding in stages while staying open.

Space for experiences

Recent shopping centers increasingly devote more floor area to places that don't sell anything, such as event plazas, play areas and sports facilities. The aim is to give people a reason to come and to make them stay longer.

Sendai's new Izumi Ward Office is completed, linked to a subway station and built by a developer for the city to buy

In Sendai, the largest city in Japan's Tohoku region, the new six-story, two-basement Izumi Ward Office has been completed, with a ceremony held on November 2. It is scheduled to open in January 2027.

The new building for the Izumi Ward Office in Sendai, the largest city in the Tohoku region of northeastern Japan, has been completed, and a completion ceremony was held on November 2. A ward office is the local branch of city hall where residents handle everyday paperwork. The new building has six floors above ground and two basement levels, and connects directly to Izumi-Chuo Station on the Sendai Subway Namboku Line. It is scheduled to open in January 2027.

The project is run by Mitsubishi Estate, and construction began in December 2024. Under the arrangement, the private developer builds the new office and the city buys it once it is finished.

Next, the old building will be demolished, and parking and a public plaza for residents will be built in its place. Private development is also planned on part of the site, and the whole block is scheduled for completion in fiscal 2029.

Briefing
  • When Mitsubishi Estate announced the start of construction in December 2024, completion was planned for the end of October 2026. According to reports, the work was finished almost exactly on that schedule.
  • Reports differ on the opening, with some saying the office will start being used in mid-January and others saying it opens at the end of January, so the exact date will need to come from the city.
  • Demolition of the old building is reported to begin in February, after the new office opens.

Background
Failed tenders for city halls

With construction costs rising, failed tenders for local government office buildings have been piling up in Japan. In October, Nasushiobara issued a tender notice for its new city hall for the third time, and Kishiwada switched to ordering design and construction separately after its design-build tender failed. The choice of procurement method is increasingly deciding whether a government building project succeeds.

Offices at the heart of towns

Shizuoka City has proposed moving its Shimizu branch office into a private mixed-use building, and similar moves are spreading across Japan to combine government offices with private development so they serve as hubs for the town, rather than standing as separate buildings.
