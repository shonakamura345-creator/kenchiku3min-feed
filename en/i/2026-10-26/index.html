Maglev's Kanagawa Station opens its track level, about 30 m (98 ft) deep, to the public for the first time

On Oct 24 and 25, the third basement level, or track level, of the Chuo Shinkansen maglev line's Kanagawa Station (working name), now under construction, was opened to the general public for the first time. Station construction is targeted for completion around spring 2030.

JR Central, Kanagawa Prefecture and Sagamihara City held a public event, Sagamihara Linear Festa 2026, on Oct 24 and 25 at the construction site of Kanagawa Station (working name) on the Chuo Shinkansen, the maglev line being built between Tokyo and Nagoya, in Sagamihara's Midori Ward. The highlight was the first public opening of the track level on the third basement floor, where the maglev main line and a secondary track will eventually run and the platforms will be.

Kanagawa Station is being built underground on the south side of Hashimoto Station, served by JR and Keio lines. It will be about 680 m (2,230 ft) long and up to about 50 m (164 ft) wide, with two platforms and four tracks on the third basement level, about 30 m (98 ft) below ground. It is the only intermediate station on the maglev line to be built underground, and it is being constructed by the cut-and-cover method, in which the ground is excavated downward from the surface.

In September, JR Central said it expects construction of the station to continue until around March 2030. The opening of the maglev line itself, meanwhile, is now expected in 2034 or later, partly because of the delayed start of work on the section in Shizuoka Prefecture.

Briefing
  • The venue was the construction site, a three-minute walk from Hashimoto Station on the JR and Keio lines, and the event ran from 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. on both days. Admission was free, but advance registration through a dedicated app was required.
  • Visiting the track level required a separate ticket, with a lottery held if there were too many applicants; the deadline was Oct 13. The track level could be reached only by stairs, and wheelchairs and strollers could not enter.
  • On the track level, visitors could see video projections and a photo spot with a life-size panel of a maglev train car. Music performances and other events were held on a special stage on the second basement level and on a stage at ground level.
  • Construction of the station structure began in October 2023. In February 2026, a shield tunneling machine that had been assembled at this site was shown to the press.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

It's a three-minute walk from the south exit of Hashimoto Station. Underneath, a huge cavity about 680 m (2,230 ft) long has been dug out. A depth of about 30 m (98 ft) is roughly the height of a 10-story building. Down there, four tracks and two platforms will sit side by side. When you think of an underground station, you picture narrow passageways and low ceilings, right? But this station is being built with the cut-and-cover method: the ground is dug out entirely from the surface, and the floors and columns are built afterward. Picture burying a big box in the earth. That's why the ceilings can be high and the columns spaced widely apart. What you can only see now, during construction, is the bare structure itself, before walls and ceilings are covered with finishes. Once it's complete, this space will fill up with platform floors, signs and benches. The station's construction is targeted for completion around March 2030.

Background
The only underground midway station

The maglev's terminal stations at Shinagawa in Tokyo and at Nagoya are being built underground, but Kanagawa Station is the only intermediate station that will be. Because it sits beneath the town in front of the existing station, how to connect it with the streets above is a major theme of Sagamihara's planning for the station area.

Showing work in progress

For large infrastructure projects that take many years to complete, more and more organizers are deliberately opening construction sites to keep local interest alive. This time, too, the prefecture and the city co-hosted the event, suggesting an aim to make the station a place people want to visit even before it opens.

Yokohama's Shin-Tsunashima MICCA opens Oct 28, pairing a historic old house with two timber buildings

Shin-Tsunashima MICCA, a commercial complex made up of a traditional old house and two new timber buildings, opens on Oct 28 in front of Shin-Tsunashima Station on the Tokyu Shin-Yokohama Line. It will house 21 restaurants and service businesses.

Shin-Tsunashima MICCA, a commercial complex in front of Shin-Tsunashima Station on the Tokyu Shin-Yokohama Line in Yokohama, celebrates its grand opening on Oct 28. It consists of three buildings: OMOYA, a renovated kominka, or traditional old Japanese house, known as the Iketani Family Residence main house, and two newly built two-story timber buildings, HANARE South and HANARE North. (Omoya means main house in Japanese, and hanare a detached annex.) The complex will house 21 restaurants and service businesses.

The Iketani Family Residence main house was designated in 2024 as both a Certified Historic Building and a Specified Landscape Formation Historic Building, two heritage designations of the City of Yokohama. OMOYA will be home to the bakery bistro flour+water and a bagel shop, so visitors will be able to eat inside the old house.

Briefing
  • The project team divided the work this way: Tokyu served as business consultant, Sumitomo Forestry handled design and construction of the timber commercial buildings, and the architecture firm Saisei Kenchiku Kenkyusho oversaw the project as a whole and was responsible for design and supervision of the old house's renovation.
  • Construction began in February 2025 for the renovation of the old house and in May 2025 for the new timber commercial buildings. Tokyu announced the complex's name and an "autumn opening" in August 2026, and later set the opening date of Oct 28.
  • An opening event, the MICCA Matsuri festival, is planned for Nov 3, after the opening.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Right outside the ticket gates of Shin-Tsunashima Station stands a large old house. This is the main house of the Iketani Family Residence. Yokohama's Certified Historic Building status isn't as strict as national cultural property designation. Instead, it's a system built on the idea of carefully preserving a building's exterior while it stays in use. In other words, this isn't a building you look at through a glass case. It's kept as a building where bread gets baked and customers sit down to eat. The other thing I want to point out is that the two new buildings next door are also two-story timber structures. On land in front of a station, you can get far more floor area by putting up a tall building, right? By not doing that, and lining up low timber buildings instead, the old house avoids being swallowed up by large buildings. If you're ever in Yokohama after the 28th, compare the height of the old house's roof with the roofs of the new timber buildings.

Background
Keeping an old house by the station

Shin-Tsunashima Station is on the Tokyu Shin-Yokohama Line, which opened in March 2023. Development in front of a station usually means tearing down old buildings and putting up a large one, but here the old house was placed at the center and surrounded by low-rise timber buildings.

Timber for commercial buildings

Driven by the push for decarbonization and by legal revisions encouraging the use of wood, more shops and offices are being built in timber in Japan. Placing old and new timber buildings side by side as a single complex has the advantage of making it easier to keep the scale of the streetscape.

Stirling Prize goes to Lion Green Road, 157 homes on a former parking lot in south London

Lion Green Road, a housing development in south London, has won the 2026 Stirling Prize from the Royal Institute of British Architects. The project built 157 homes on the site of a former parking lot.

On Oct 15, the Royal Institute of British Architects (RIBA) announced the 2026 winner of the RIBA Stirling Prize, awarded to the best new building in the UK. The winner is Lion Green Road, a housing development in Coulsdon, in the London Borough of Croydon in south London. It was designed by Mary Duggan Architects, working with RUFF Architects.

On the site of a former parking lot, the project placed five freestanding buildings with a total of 157 homes. It provides homes for sale alongside social housing, public rental homes with reduced rents, on the same site. The client was Brick by Brick, the Croydon council's housing company, which has since been dissolved.

This is the 30th year of the Stirling Prize. Lion Green Road is the fourth housing project to win, after Accordia in 2008, Goldsmith Street in 2019 and Appleby Blue Almshouse in 2025.

Briefing
  • Six projects made the shortlist, including Paddington Square by Renzo Piano Building Workshop, Mill Lane by Haworth Tompkins and Beam by Bennetts Associates.
  • The site slopes down about 8 m (26 ft) from back to front. The judges praised the way the project used this difference in height to create a "living landscape" that looks out over a nearby park.
  • The announcement was made at Old Billingsgate in London. Bookmakers had offered a sixfold payout on the project, and it was the first time architect Mary Duggan had been shortlisted.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

According to the bookmakers, this was the least favored of the six projects. And it won the Stirling Prize. Lion Green Road is a housing development that sets five buildings, with space between them, on a sloping site that used to be a parking lot. You know the danchi, Japan's big public housing estates, that you see from train windows, with identical blocks lined up facing the same direction, right? Here, the 8 m (26 ft) difference in height between the back and the front of the site was used to arrange the buildings so they look out over a nearby park. That's the part the judges called a "living landscape." On top of that, homes for sale and lower-rent homes are mixed on the same site. The site isn't divided up by residents' income. Could the parking lots in front of Japanese train stations and beside danchi be turned into homes like this, too? And who would make that happen, architects or local governments? What do you think?

Background
Why a housing win matters

The Stirling Prize tends to go to high-profile buildings such as museums and university facilities, and in 30 years only four housing projects have won. Three of those four have come since 2019, which can be read as a sign that growing concern about Britain's housing shortage is showing up in the judging too.

A comparison with Japan

Japan, too, faces the challenge of rebuilding aging public housing estates and making use of underused land such as parking lots. Mixing homes for sale with public rental housing on the same site is still rare in Japan, and overseas examples like this are likely to be cited more and more as references.
