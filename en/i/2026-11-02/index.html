Japan's revised building rules take effect, adding a new type approval route for material shortages

A revised Cabinet Order under Japan's Building Standards Act took effect on November 1 to keep housing supply flowing smoothly. It expands the type conformity certification system and streamlines rules on natural lighting, fire safety and hazardous materials.

A Cabinet Order amending the Order for Enforcement of the Building Standards Act, Japan's national building code, took effect on November 1. The amendment, led by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT), was approved by the Cabinet on September 11 and promulgated on September 16. Its aim is to support a smooth supply of housing and the introduction of new equipment while keeping buildings safe.

The centerpiece is an expansion of type conformity certification, a system in which a standardized design is certified in advance as meeting the code, so that parts of the review can be skipped in building confirmation, Japan's pre-construction check that a design complies with the Building Standards Act. Until now there were three categories: the entire building, the building excluding its building services (mechanical and electrical systems), and building services alone. A new category has been added that covers compliance with the provisions on structural strength. If a particular building material becomes hard to get, the structural review can still be skipped, and only the parts involving the substitute material are checked individually.

Three other sets of rules were also revised. Among child welfare facilities and similar buildings, those designated by the MLIT minister as places where users stay only briefly and there is no hygiene concern are now exempt from the natural lighting rules, which require a minimum window area in certain rooms. A path has opened to exempt buildings with a certain level of fire resistance from fire safety and evacuation rules. And under the caps on the total amount of hazardous materials allowed in each zoning district, fuel gas equipment that meets the minister's standards is now excluded.

Briefing
  • The order was approved by the Cabinet on September 11 and promulgated on September 16. The government had also announced that related ministerial notices and amendments to the Building Standards Act's enforcement regulations would be issued at the end of October and take effect on November 1.
  • The exemptions from the natural lighting rules and from the fire safety and evacuation rules apply only when standards set by the MLIT minister are met. The specific standards are laid out in ministerial notices, so practitioners will need to check those notices before making decisions.
  • The revised cap on hazardous materials is meant to encourage the installation of equipment that runs on compressed or flammable gas as fuel. It is a measure to make it easier to place new energy equipment in zoning districts, including residential ones.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Get the whole recipe certified by the government in advance. That's basically what type conformity certification is. Homebuilders that put up lots of houses with the same floor plan and the same structure get approval up front that this type meets the code, and then they can skip part of the review each time they apply for a permit on an individual house. The trouble comes when one ingredient becomes unavailable, right? Until now, swapping out even part of the recipe could undermine the whole premise that it had been certified. With this amendment, builders can get just the structural part certified as a type and have only the substituted material reviewed individually. Here's the catch, though: what you save is time spent on the review, not the checks at the site. I think having someone actually look at whether the house is built as certified becomes even more important. With this change, there are now four categories of certification.

Background
Type approval and mass-produced homes

Type conformity certification has been used by builders who repeatedly put up houses to the same specifications, as a way to make reviews more efficient. But changing any part of the specification could undermine the basis for the certification, which made it hard to switch material suppliers. The new category partly loosens that rigidity.

Streamlining continues

Around the Building Standards Act, amendments that tighten reviews, such as the April 2025 scaling back of the so-called Category 4 exemption, which had let small wooden houses designed by a licensed architect skip parts of the review, are moving forward alongside amendments that streamline procedures and rules. This one is the latter kind: it leaves the safety standards themselves unchanged and revises how reviews are carried out and where the rules apply.

Illegal buildings still stand at Sapporo's North Safari zoo after the removal deadline passes

The deadline to remove buildings put up without permission in a zone where construction is restricted passed on October 31. With animals and structures still on site, the city of Sapporo inspects on November 2 and is also considering a criminal complaint.

The deadline set in a demolition order issued by the city of Sapporo, the largest city on the northern island of Hokkaido, passed on October 31 for illegal buildings at North Safari Sapporo, a zoo in the city's Minami Ward. Several TV stations reported that animal enclosures and other buildings were still standing on the former zoo grounds when the deadline arrived.

The facility built animal housing and other structures without permission in an urbanization control area, where construction is in principle not allowed, and operated there for about 20 years. On March 23, the city ordered the operating company to remove all 37 illegal buildings by the end of October. At the city's on-site inspection on October 9, 25 buildings were reportedly still standing.

What makes removal difficult is the animals still on the grounds. As of the end of September, about 220 animals remained. The city is conducting an on-site inspection today, November 2, to check on the buildings and the animals, and says it may consider steps such as filing a criminal complaint depending on what it finds.

Briefing
  • The city's March demolition order covered 37 buildings. The city said 25 remained at its October 9 inspection, but the operating company's representative has put the number of remaining buildings at 22, so the figures don't match.
  • Care of the remaining animals has been taken over by Hokkaido Animal World. The company is planning a new protective care facility, with three candidate sites near Sapporo, but land negotiations are reportedly taking time.
  • The operating company's representative told reporters that immediate removal would be difficult and that even finishing within the year would be hard.

“These are buildings with animals actually living in them, so there's a limit to how fast we can remove them.” That's what the operator has argued. And that's what makes this case so difficult. Normally, correcting an illegal building is over once the building comes down. But here, the animal housing itself is home to about 220 living creatures. Before you can demolish it, you have to build somewhere for them to move. The company that took over the animals' care is planning a new facility, but negotiations for candidate sites have reportedly run into trouble. An urbanization control area is a zone where a line has been drawn saying that, as a rule, nothing gets built here, so that cities don't sprawl endlessly. In Japan, it's thanks to that line that the farmland and woods near people's homes don't suddenly turn into housing developments. For about 20 years, the zoo kept operating on the restricted side of that line. The longer a violation goes on, the more time and money it takes to undo. What should a system for catching and stopping violations early look like?

Background
What an urbanization control area is

Japan's City Planning Act divides urban areas into urbanization promotion areas, where development is actively encouraged, and urbanization control areas, where it is held back. Building in a control area generally requires a development permit or similar approval, and unpermitted construction is subject to enforcement. The system protects farmland and forests and keeps the area that roads and sewers must serve from spreading too far.

Enforcement's last resort

If an owner does not comply with a demolition order, the government can file a criminal complaint or move to administrative execution by proxy, in which it removes the structures itself in place of the owner. But execution by proxy is costly and labor-intensive, and it is not often used in practice. Here, because animals are inside the buildings, somewhere to move them has to be secured first, which makes the process more complicated than ordinary enforcement against illegal buildings.

Moreau Kusunoki's Powerhouse Parramatta museum opens November 7 in western Sydney

A new museum of about 30,000 m² (323,000 sq ft) opens on November 7 in Parramatta, in western Sydney. It was designed by Moreau Kusunoki, a Paris-based practice formed by French and Japanese architects.

Powerhouse Parramatta, a new museum in Parramatta in western Sydney, Australia, opens on November 7. The project was developed by the New South Wales state government, and the building has a total floor area of about 30,000 m² (323,000 sq ft).

Moreau Kusunoki, a Paris-based practice formed by a French architect and a Japanese architect, served as lead designer, with the Australian firm Genton joining as local architect. Moreau Kusunoki was selected through an international design competition in 2019, and completion was originally planned for 2025. Built by Lendlease, the building was completed in April 2026, and since then work has continued on installing the exhibitions and finishing the surrounding public spaces.

Five exhibitions open with the museum. For two of them, “Task Eternal” and “The Mall,” the exhibition design was supported by AMO, the research arm of OMA, the firm founded by Rem Koolhaas, and by OPEN Architecture, an architecture firm from China.

Briefing
  • Parramatta is a city some distance west of central Sydney. The Powerhouse is a museum whose main site is in Ultimo, in central Sydney. The new museum was delivered by the state government's infrastructure agency.
  • ArchDaily and the Japanese art magazine Bijutsu Techo report the total floor area as about 30,000 m² (323,000 sq ft), while the state government's project page gives the museum portion as 18,000 m² (194,000 sq ft). The difference appears to come from how the space is counted.
  • The opening exhibitions include “Task Eternal,” “The Mall” and “The Dark.” The festival marking the opening also lists the two architects of Moreau Kusunoki among its speakers.

A fine lattice framework wraps the whole outside of the building. Moreau Kusunoki, the practice behind it, is a duo based in Paris: Nicolas Moreau from France and Hiroko Kusunoki from Japan. When Japanese architects win big public projects in competitions abroad, the famous examples are Kengo Kuma and SANAA, right? But actually, there are quite a few teams based in Europe where Japanese and non-Japanese architects join forces to compete worldwide. An exoskeleton like this means you don't hide the columns and beams inside the building; you put them out on the exterior. Picture a crab's shell and it's easy to understand. Since the shell holds up the body, the inside is free to use however you like. That's a good fit for a building like a museum, which keeps swapping large exhibits in and out. Seven years since the 2019 competition. I think we're in an era when the names of architects with ties to Japan show up on major facilities overseas.

Background
Culture in the suburbs

Powerhouse Parramatta was planned to put a large museum not in the city center, where cultural facilities tend to cluster, but in the growing western suburbs. Building cultural hubs away from the center to shift a city's center of gravity is an approach seen in major cities in many countries.

Architects design the shows, too

Architecture firms helped design two of the opening exhibitions. Architects shaping not just the building but also the exhibition spaces inside it has become more common at art museums and museums in recent years.
