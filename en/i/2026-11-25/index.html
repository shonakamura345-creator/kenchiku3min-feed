Redevelopment behind Shibuya Hikarie in Tokyo wins key approval for a 38-story tower and bus terminal due in 2035

The redevelopment of the Shibuya 2-chome West district, on the east side of Shibuya Station in Tokyo, received approval for its rights-conversion plan as of Nov 24. A 38-story tower with a bus terminal is targeted for completion in 2035.

Tokyo Tatemono, the Urban Renaissance Agency (UR, a government-backed urban development body) and the Shibuya 2-chome West District Urban Redevelopment Association said they received approval from the Governor of Tokyo, as of Nov 24, for the rights-conversion plan of the Shibuya 2-chome West District Type 1 Urban Redevelopment Project, on the east side of Shibuya Station. The project is an urban redevelopment project, a Japanese scheme in which landowners pool their plots to build a larger complex. The rights-conversion plan sets out how the land and building rights that owners hold today will be swapped for floor space and other rights in the finished building, and it is a major milestone before a redevelopment can move into construction.

The core of the plan is the tower on Block B: 3 floors below ground and 38 above, about 192 m (630 ft) tall, with a total floor area of about 235,000 m² (2.5 million sq ft). It will hold offices, shops, a hotel and a training center, plus a five-bay bus terminal (about 5,000 m² (54,000 sq ft) including support facilities) for long-distance highway buses and airport buses. Tour buses will also be able to use it.

After the existing buildings are demolished, main construction is set to begin in 2029, with completion targeted for 2035. Block A, which faces Miyamasuzaka, a sloping street that climbs east from Shibuya Station, will have an elevated plaza and shops, and Sou Fujimoto Architects is serving as design architect.

Briefing
  • In the plan announced in 2022, the Block B tower had 4 floors below ground and 41 above, a maximum height of about 208 m (680 ft) and a total floor area of about 255,000 m² (2.7 million sq ft), with completion targeted for fiscal 2029. The current plan lowers the floor count and height, and completion has also slipped later.
  • The redevelopment association was approved in January 2023. At that time, the plan was to obtain approval for the rights-conversion plan in fiscal 2024 and to start construction in fiscal 2025.
  • The project is being carried out as the Shibuya REGENERATION Project, which combines the urban redevelopment project for Blocks A and B with a joint rebuilding of Block C led by Tokyo Tatemono. The total site area is about 18,700 m² (201,000 sq ft), and it is described as the largest redevelopment in the Shibuya area.
  • The plaza is planned with plenty of greenery and large eaves, as one of the few large places to relax around Shibuya Station.

Walk up Miyamasuzaka from the east exit of Shibuya Station and you come out behind Hikarie. Yesterday, a plan to build a 38-story tower about 192 m (630 ft) tall on this corner moved one step forward. The rights-conversion plan that Tokyo approved is a bit like a room-assignment chart for a move. Which floor in the new building does each owner get in exchange for the land or building they hold now? Until that's settled, nothing can be torn down and nothing can be built. What I'm watching is the bus terminal inside the tower. Highway buses and airport buses will leave from five bays. Rolling a suitcase through hilly Shibuya is pretty tiring, right? If the bus stops are gathered into one building, the whole flow of people on the station's east side changes. The elevated plaza facing the slope is being designed by Sou Fujimoto's office. Completion is planned for 2035. A child starting first grade today will be in the final year of junior high by then.

Background
Redevelopment moves uphill

Around Shibuya Station, a series of high-rise rebuilds has gone up directly above or in front of the station, including Shibuya Hikarie in 2012 and the East Tower of Shibuya Scramble Square in 2019. This site sits at the top of Miyamasuzaka, so redevelopment is spreading one step away from the station, up the slope.

Pushed-back schedules

Amid soaring construction costs, redevelopments across Japan have been scaled back or delayed one after another. As we reported on Nov 13 and Nov 20, the plans in front of Hakodate Station and at the north exit of JR Fuji Station were restarted at a smaller size. The bigger the project, the harder it is to predict the time and cost before construction begins.

Construction begins on a 20-story, 286-room hotel on Aioi-dori in Hiroshima, with opening set for 2029

Marubeni Urban Development and Tokyu Land Corporation said on Nov 24 that main construction has begun on a 20-story hotel in the Kanayamacho area of Naka Ward, Hiroshima. It will have 286 rooms and is scheduled to open in 2029.

Marubeni Urban Development and Tokyu Land Corporation said on Nov 24 that main construction has started on their joint project, provisionally named the Hiroshima Naka Ward Kanayamacho Hotel Project. The steel-framed, 20-story hotel will stand on a site facing Aioi-dori, a main avenue in central Hiroshima served by streetcars, a one-minute walk from the Kanayamacho stop on the Hiroden Main Line.

The hotel is planned to have 286 rooms, mainly doubles and twins, plus quad rooms that sleep four. It will be operated by Solare Hotels & Resorts and is scheduled to open in 2029.

Briefing
  • Guest rooms will range from 21.60 to 56.92 m² (232 to 613 sq ft).
  • The address is 4-1 Kanayamacho and other lots, Naka Ward, Hiroshima. The site is about 1,185 m² (12,800 sq ft), zoned commercial, with a floor area ratio of 900%.
  • The design is by Hazama Ando Corporation.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

A floor area ratio of 900%. That's the cap on floor space allowed on this hotel's site. It means you can stack up floors totaling nine times the area of the land. The site is about 1,185 m² (12,800 sq ft). That's roughly four and a half tennis courts. On it, guest rooms will be stacked up to the 20th floor to create 286 rooms. Picture a flat piece of land being stretched upward, right? Such a high ratio is allowed in commercial districts downtown to gather people and buildings close to stations and streetcar stops. Running in front of the building is the streetcar line that serves as a main way of getting around Hiroshima. At a one-minute walk from the stop, even travelers with big bags can find their way without trouble. The main avenues you walk every day are probably the same. Not just big redevelopments, but rebuilding one building at a time changes the face of a street. I think that kind of accumulation is how cities are made.

Background
A streetcar city

Hiroshima Electric Railway's streetcars run one of the largest networks of their kind in Japan and are an everyday way of getting around for residents. In 2025, a new route also opened that brings the streetcars directly into the second floor of the Hiroshima Station building. Gathering hotels and shops near the stops is a way of building that fits well with this kind of transit-centered city planning.

Renovation contractor bankruptcies in Japan hit 108 through October, already a record for a full year

According to Tokyo Shoko Research, bankruptcies in the home renovation industry reached 108 from January to October 2026, already passing the 105 recorded in 2025, the highest annual total on record.

Tokyo Shoko Research, a Japanese credit research firm, said on Nov 23 that bankruptcies among renovation contractors reached 108 from January to October 2026. That means the 105 cases of 2025 (January to December), the highest annual total since 2002, were passed in just ten months.

Demand for renovation itself is growing, as soaring housing prices have made existing homes more popular. At the same time, the firm says many of the bankruptcies are at small and very small businesses that cannot fully pass rising material and labor costs on to their prices and end up having to take on loss-making jobs.

Businesses with fewer than five employees accounted for 86 cases, about 80% of the total. The firm says the full-year figure could reach 130.

Briefing
  • As of the end of September (January to September), there were 96 bankruptcies, 20.0% more than a year earlier, already the most since 2002 at that point.
  • In a Teikoku Databank survey (released Nov 17), 53.6% of all companies said they were short of full-time employees as of October, rising to 68.9% in construction.
  • Teikoku Databank counted 225 bankruptcies caused by labor shortages in the first half of fiscal 2026, a record for the fourth year in a row, and construction had the most of any industry with 70.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

If you were going to have your home renovated, what kind of company would you picture? Rather than a big homebuilder, I think many people picture a local builder or a small company with a handful of tradespeople. This year, bankruptcies among those renovation companies reached 108 through October. That's a record. And 80% were businesses with fewer than five employees. It may sound surprising, but it's not that the work has dried up. New homes have become expensive, and more people are buying existing homes and fixing them up. The pain is in the prices. Say a company took on a bathroom replacement based on a quote from the spring. By the time the work began, both materials and the tradespeople's wages had gone up. The company has no choice but to absorb the difference. The smaller the company, the harder that hits. There's something the customer can do, too. Next time you get a quote for renovation work, check in the contract how much you've agreed to pay before the work starts.

Background
Who handles the resale shift

As we reported on Nov 21, the average price of a new condo in the Tokyo area was ¥93.08 million in October. The harder new homes are to afford, the more people choose to buy an existing home and work on it. Small local renovation companies and tradespeople are the ones doing that work.

Watch the deposits

In an analysis of the first half of 2025, Tokyo Shoko Research pointed out that more than 90% of bankruptcies in the renovation and painting industries were liquidations, and that advance payments for unfinished work are hard to get back. The more a contract requires you to pay before work begins, the bigger the impact if the company goes under.
