780-unit TOKYO BLUE LINC starts on university land in Tokyo's Monzen-Nakacho on a 70-year lease

Nomura Real Estate Development has launched a 780-unit mixed-use project on about 1.7 hectares (4.2 acres) owned by the Institute of Science Tokyo. Built on a land lease of about 70 years, it combines condos for sale, senior housing and shops.

Nomura Real Estate Development announced on Oct 16 that it has launched TOKYO BLUE LINC, a large mixed-use development of housing and retail with 780 units in total, in Etchujima, in Tokyo's Koto Ward on the east side of the city. The site, about 1.7 hectares (4.2 acres), belongs to the Institute of Science Tokyo (formerly Tokyo Medical and Dental University). Nomura is developing it on its own under a general fixed-term land lease of about 70 years, a Japanese lease under which the land goes back to the owner when the term ends.

The plan comprises Proud City Monzen-Nakacho, a condominium for sale; OUKAS Monzen-Nakacho, a rental senior residence; a commercial facility; and a shared building that local residents can also use. Of the 780 units, 620 are for sale, 47 are not for sale and 113 are in the senior residence. The commercial facility is planned to include a supermarket.

The condominium is scheduled for completion in late December 2027, and the commercial facility and community building are expected to open in fiscal 2028. The nearest stations are Etchujima, Monzen-Nakacho and Tsukishima, giving access to four train lines.

Briefing
  • The buildings will rise 19 stories, with the housing split across four buildings. Handover of the for-sale units is scheduled for late March 2028.
  • OUKAS Monzen-Nakacho, the 113-unit senior residence, will be run by Nomura Real Estate Wellness, part of the Nomura Real Estate group. It is scheduled for completion in late March 2028.
  • According to the university, Tokyo Medical and Dental University (as it was then) and Nomura signed a project agreement and a contract establishing the general fixed-term land lease for its Etchujima land in March 2024.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

About 70 years. That's how long Nomura Real Estate Development will lease this land. The owner is the Institute of Science Tokyo, and the deal is that when the term ends, the land is in principle returned as a cleared lot. That's how a “general fixed-term land lease” works. Think of it as the contract period on a rental apartment, attached not to the unit but to the ground itself. People who buy a condo here will own their apartment, but the land under their feet is borrowed from the university. Because of that, prices tend to be lower than for condos that come with land in the same spot. On the other hand, the building's “last day” is set from the start. If your home came with an end date written on it, it would give you pause, wouldn't it? But really, for most people, 70 years is a whole lifetime. The university earns income without giving up its land, and the neighborhood gains homes and places to shop. That's the deal. The condominium is scheduled to be finished in December 2027.

Background
Leasehold condos

A general fixed-term land lease rents out land for a set term of 50 years or more; when the term ends, the contract is not renewed and the land goes back to the owner. Because buyers don't purchase the land, condo prices are easier to keep down, which makes it easier to supply homes even in prime central locations. On the other hand, resale prices are said to fall more easily as the remaining term gets shorter.

Putting university land to work

National universities across Japan are increasingly leasing their land to private companies and putting the income toward teaching and research. On this Etchujima project, the Tokyo Shimbun newspaper reported in 2024 that local residents had raised concerns about its effect on the cityscape, and that the backdrop was the national government's push for universities to make use of their land.

Yokohama sets a plan to bring a library and everyday life to Shin-Yokohama Station's north side

Yokohama has drawn up a community development policy for the area north of Shin-Yokohama Station. It would link the station by deck to a planned new library of about 20,000 m² (215,000 sq ft) at the north exit and aims to attract supermarkets and other shops.

The City of Yokohama on Oct 15 published a community development policy for the area north of Shin-Yokohama Station, the Shinkansen bullet train stop that serves Yokohama. The policy covers an area that includes the numbered blocks of Shin-Yokohama 1 through 3 in Kohoku Ward, where a land readjustment project was completed in 1975. Under the vision “a city center where gathering creates value and connections move the future,” it sets a direction of strengthening transport functions and making more intensive use of land.

One pillar of the policy is linking up with a new municipal library planned on city-owned land at the station's north exit. Options under consideration include a new pedestrian deck to make it easier for people to move between the station and the library. The new library will have a total floor area of about 20,000 m² (215,000 sq ft) and is expected to be completed in seven to 10 years.

An outline of the policy was presented in February after workshops with residents, and the policy has now been finalized. In response to residents' input, it also calls for attracting supermarkets and other facilities needed for daily life.

Briefing
  • The Yokohama City Board of Education drew up a basic concept for the new library in March 2026. It plans a collection of about 1 million books and about 1,000 seats, including reading seats.
  • The library site was chosen after comparing the areas around five stations, including Yokohama and Higashi-Kanagawa. The city-owned land at Shin-Yokohama's north exit won out on factors such as ease of access from across the city.
  • The Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, a construction trade daily, reported on the policy on Oct 16, describing it as focused on getting people to move around between the station area and the new library.

Background
Land readjustment towns

Land readjustment (kukaku seiri) is a method that reorganizes tangled, irregular plots and builds roads and parks all at once. Towns laid out to match the opening of a Shinkansen station, like Shin-Yokohama, have large, easy-to-use roads and blocks, but they tend to become office-centered, and everyday life struggles to take root.

A library by the station

More and more cities across Japan are placing large libraries right by their stations to create flows of people through town. Facilities that draw people every day for reasons other than shopping are expected to send visitors on to nearby shops and cafes.

Snøhetta's Shanghai Grand Opera House opens with a fan-shaped roof anyone can climb year-round

The Shanghai Grand Opera House, designed by Norwegian firm Snøhetta, opened on Oct 17 along the Huangpu River. Its spiraling roof, reminiscent of a folding fan, is open to the public.

The Shanghai Grand Opera House, a new opera house on the banks of the Huangpu River in Shanghai, China, opened on Oct 17. It was designed by Norwegian architecture firm Snøhetta, which was selected in a 2016 international design competition and formally commissioned in 2019. The firm worked with partners including the Chinese design institute ECADI, and completion was originally planned for 2025.

The building has a total floor area of about 146,800 m² (1.58 million sq ft) and contains three theaters: a main hall of about 2,000 seats, a hall of about 1,200 seats and a flexible stage of about 1,000 seats. The spiraling roof, inspired by the traditional Chinese folding fan, will be open year-round as a public rooftop where people can climb up and look out over the city and the river.

On the evening of the opening, a celebratory concert was held to coincide with the start of the 25th China Shanghai International Arts Festival. Puccini's Tosca was chosen as the first opera of the opening season.

Briefing
  • Japan's Nagata Acoustics worked on the acoustic design of the main hall. Theater planning was handled by Theatre Projects of the UK.
  • Inside, a large spiral staircase leads up to the roof. Chinese media have likened this double-helix staircase to a dancer's skirt.
  • According to Chinese media, the opening day was also the first day of the 25th China Shanghai International Arts Festival, and the Shanghai Performing Arts Group was launched the same day. The outdoor fan-shaped rooftop terrace is reportedly set to reopen to the public by reservation from late October.

Background
A lineage of walkable roofs

Snøhetta is known for treating buildings like landforms and turning roofs into public spaces people can walk on. The Oslo Opera House, which opened in 2008, is the best-known example, and the opera house under construction in Busan, South Korea, is also planned with a walkable roof. The idea is to make the theater a city plaza where people gather even on days without performances.

Japanese acoustics abroad

In acoustic design, the field of shaping how concert halls and opera houses sound, Japanese firms have worked on many of the world's major halls. It's not unusual for Japanese expertise to be behind the scenes at large theaters overseas.
