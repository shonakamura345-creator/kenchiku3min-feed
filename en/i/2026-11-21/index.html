Umekita Forest opens at Grand Green Osaka, bringing a waterfall and pond to Osaka Station

On Nov 20, the Umekita Forest, about 8,400 m² (90,000 sq ft) of woodland, opened at Grand Green Osaka north of JR Osaka Station. A pedestrian deck linking the project's north and south parks also opened in full the same day.

At noon on Nov 20, the Umekita Forest opened in part of the Umekita Park North Park at Grand Green Osaka, the redevelopment underway on the north side of JR Osaka Station, the main rail hub of Japan's second-largest city. The forest covers about 8,400 m² (90,000 sq ft). It was originally scheduled to open around spring 2027, but the opening was brought forward based on how construction had progressed.

More than 100 species of trees and plants have been planted, centered on cherry, maple and katsura, and the forest includes water features such as a waterfall and a pond. Plants native to the Osaka area were also included, with the aim of creating a natural environment that supports wildlife on land that was once the Umeda freight yard. A cafe restaurant overlooking the forest opened the same day.

Also on Nov 20, the Hirameki no Michi, a pedestrian deck connecting the north and south parks, opened in full. The remaining area of about 2,200 m² (24,000 sq ft), across a road, is scheduled to open around spring 2027.

Briefing
  • The North Park site covers about 10,000 m² (108,000 sq ft), and about 8,400 m² (90,000 sq ft) of it opened ahead of the rest. The forest opened to the public after an opening ceremony at noon.
  • The planting includes deciduous trees such as Chinese hackberry and muku trees, along with species suited to waterside settings. Surrounding the forest with the waterfall and pond is meant to block views and noise from outside, creating a quiet place in the middle of the city.
  • Events including a drone show are scheduled through Nov 23 to mark the opening.

Background
Redeveloping a freight yard

The Umekita district sits on the site of the former Umeda freight yard north of JR Osaka Station. Grand Front Osaka opened in 2013 on the first area to be developed, and the second-phase area to its west has been built out as Grand Green Osaka. The second phase partly opened in September 2024, and it is notable for placing a large park at the center of the plan.

A park at the station

Land in front of big-city terminal stations is expensive, so the usual approach is to stack up floor space for offices and shops. Grand Green Osaka instead puts a large park at the heart of the district and arranges offices, hotels and housing around it. It is a leading example of a development that promotes the amount of green space itself as the value of the neighborhood.

Kochi's new prefectural gymnasium design moves parking above ground over tsunami fears

On Nov 19, Kochi Prefecture on the island of Shikoku released an outline of the design proposal for its new prefectural gymnasium. Responding to concerns about tsunami flooding, the plan puts at least 250 parking spaces above ground instead of underground.

Kochi Prefecture, on the Pacific coast of Shikoku, announced on Nov 19 an outline of the proposal from the designer of its planned new prefectural gymnasium. The design is being handled by a joint venture of Azusa Sekkei and Miyabi Sekkei, a firm based in the city of Kochi, under the concept of a "living room for the city." The prefecture aims to open the facility in the middle of fiscal 2031.

The biggest change is the parking. The basic plan the prefecture compiled in June included the option of underground parking, but after residents voiced concerns about tsunami flooding, the proposal secures at least 250 spaces above ground. The building will use a pilotis structure, raised on columns, with the space underneath used for parking.

The three-story building will include an arena with seating for more than 5,000 spectators and a martial arts hall, among other spaces. The sub-arena is being moved up to the second floor and grouped with the martial arts hall and other facilities on the same level, which is also intended to make the building easier to use. The details may change during design, and the design is expected to be finalized around summer 2027.

Briefing
  • The outline of the basic plan the prefecture released in April estimated a total floor area of about 17,000 to 19,000 m² (183,000 to 205,000 sq ft), excluding the pilotis and underground parking. It assumed more than 5,000 seats in the main arena and about 500 to 1,000 seats in the sub-arena.
  • At the prefecture's review committee, members said underground parking should be avoided because of the risk and cost. In the public comment process, residents also opposed underground parking because of the risk of tsunami flooding.
  • The designer was chosen through an open design proposal competition, and the prefecture published the results of the second round of review in October. The prefecture wants a facility that can also host professional sports, concerts and exhibitions.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Leave the ground floor open, with only columns, and keep the space underneath free. That's called pilotis. Think of the raised-floor storehouses of ancient Japan you may have seen in a textbook, right? It's a similar idea. Kochi's new prefectural gymnasium will put parking under its pilotis. At first, there was actually an option to build the parking underground. But the worry was a tsunami. A basement is the lowest point on the water's path. Once water gets in, it won't drain until it's pumped out. So the cars stay above ground, and important floors like the sub-arena move up to the second floor. Even for a big building seating more than 5,000 people, the first question is: what happens when the water comes? In your town too, the gym is often where people evacuate during a disaster. Reworking the plan in response to residents' voices was, I think, a sensible decision for public architecture in a city near the sea. Designs that don't rely on basements may become the new standard.

Background
Basements and flooding

Underground parking and electrical rooms tend to fill with water when flooded, and draining and repairing them takes time. In 2019, Typhoon Hagibis flooded the basement electrical equipment of a high-rise condominium in Musashi-Kosugi, Kawasaki, causing prolonged power and water outages. Since then, interest has grown in designs that assume flooding, such as placing electrical equipment on upper floors.

Arenas across Japan

Large arenas that can host professional sports and concerts are being built one after another across Japan. Seating for more than 5,000 people is large for a gymnasium in a regional city. Because these buildings host sports and events day to day and serve as evacuation shelters in a disaster, their design has to meet both disaster-preparedness and visitor-drawing goals.

Average price of new Tokyo-area condos falls for a third month to ¥93.08 million in October

According to the Real Estate Economic Institute, the average price of new condominiums released in the Tokyo area in October was ¥93.08 million, down 5.9% from a year earlier. It was the third straight monthly decline.

According to the October survey of the Tokyo-area new condominium market released on Nov 19 by the Real Estate Economic Institute, a private research firm that tracks housing sales, the average price per unit was ¥93.08 million, down 5.9% from the same month a year earlier. It was the third consecutive month below the previous year's level.

In Tokyo's 23 wards, the city's urban core, the average price was ¥129.37 million, down 15.5% year on year and the first decline in four months. The reasons cited were the absence of large, high-priced projects in central Tokyo, along with rising interest rates that made prospective buyers more hesitant, which in turn led sellers to cut the number of units released.

Even so, the institute says that while demand is expected to fall because of rising interest rates and other factors, construction costs continue to soar, making further declines unlikely.

Briefing
  • The survey covers Tokyo and the three surrounding prefectures of Kanagawa, Saitama and Chiba. The average price depends on the mix of projects released that month, so it does not mean the price of the same unit went down.
  • Mortgage rates are moving too. MUFG Bank says it will revise the base rate for its variable-rate home loans from Dec 1, following the increase in its short-term prime rate announced on Sep 18.
Sources

¥93.08 million. That's the average price per unit of new condos put on sale in the Tokyo area in October. It's 5.9% cheaper than a year earlier. When you hear prices fell, you think, "Maybe it's time to buy," right? But look inside the number, and it's a little different. It's like the average spend at a conveyor-belt sushi restaurant dropping on the one day the fatty tuna sells out. Meanwhile, the cost of building stays high. The institute doesn't expect prices to keep sliding from here. Even when sales slow, the builders' costs don't come down. The condo prices you see are sitting in the middle of a tug-of-war between the people buying now and the people building. Will rising rates make buyers more cautious, or will construction costs settle and give sellers some room? In this tug-of-war, which side will ease up first?

Background
Costs hold prices up

The price of a new condo is built up from the land cost, construction cost, sales expenses and so on. Because construction costs keep rising with materials and labor, it is hard to cut prices sharply even when sales slow. As we reported on Nov 20, the scaling-back of the redevelopment in front of Fuji Station also stems from the same surge in construction costs.

Rates hit monthly payments

For a home loan with a variable rate, rising rates increase both the monthly payment and the total amount repaid. The bigger the loan, as is common for central Tokyo properties, the more even a small change in rates affects a household budget. When buyers become cautious, sellers tend to cut the number of units released and wait to see how the market moves.
