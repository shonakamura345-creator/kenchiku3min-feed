Japan maps out BIM data review for building permits, aiming for a spring 2029 start

At a panel meeting on October 28, Japan's construction ministry laid out its next steps toward a building confirmation system that reviews the BIM data itself, with a target start in spring 2029.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) held the 25th meeting of its Building BIM Environment Development Subcommittee on October 28 and laid out its next steps toward launching BIM data review, in which the BIM data itself becomes the subject of review in building confirmation applications. Building confirmation is Japan's pre-construction check that a design complies with the Building Standards Act. MLIT aims to start the system in spring 2029 and plans to finalize the review and inspection methods for each covered item by the end of fiscal 2026, which ends in March 2027.

Because it would be difficult to check every item of building confirmation with BIM data at launch, the ministry is choosing which items to prioritize based on the balance between the effort of data entry and the benefits. According to the trade newspaper Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun, items under consideration include the building coverage ratio, the floor area ratio (FAR) and fire compartments. Items not reviewed with data are expected to be reviewed using PDF drawings, as they are today.

BIM-based building confirmation is being introduced in stages. The first stage, BIM drawing review, began in April 2026. In that stage, what gets reviewed is still the drawings, and the BIM data is treated only as a reference. BIM data review in 2029 is the next stage, in which the data itself is used in the review.

Briefing
  • The study of BIM data review is being carried out by a review task force set up by MLIT. Under it are four working groups: one on zoning-type rules that govern a building's relationship to its surroundings, one on fire-protection rules, one on other provisions and one on rules for building services.
  • For FAR review, the plan is to first separate items that can be settled one way from the data from items that require the judgment of designers or reviewers, and then sort out which information should be held in the IFC data and which should come from outside sources. IFC is a common format for exchanging data between different BIM software programs.
  • As for review methods, the ministry has presented a concept that combines “view review,” in which the data is checked in displays tailored to each review item, with “digital review,” in which it is checked by programs or AI. The approach is also expected to reduce inconsistency between reviews.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Spring 2029. In about two and a half years, what actually gets reviewed in building confirmation will start to change. The stack of drawings for a permit application is surprisingly thick, isn't it? Right now, the reviewers read through it one sheet at a time. BIM is data in which a building is assembled inside the computer, along with information for each part, like columns, walls and rooms. If drawings are like a picture of what the finished building will look like, BIM is closer to a plastic model kit with the insides filled in. BIM data review means the reviewers receive that model directly and examine it. What I find interesting about this round of discussion is that they're trying to separate things that a calculation settles one way, like the floor area ratio, from things that need human judgment. They're not handing everything over to machines. Counting floor area goes to the data, and the parts open to interpretation go to the reviewers. The review methods will be finalized at the end of this fiscal year, in March 2027.

Background
A roadmap in stages

MLIT has laid out a plan to bring BIM into building confirmation step by step. A 2024 subcommittee meeting set the policy of starting BIM drawing review in spring 2026 and BIM data review in spring 2029, and the first stage, BIM drawing review, began this April. This latest meeting comes as the ministry works out the details of the next stage.

What it means for design firms

Once the data itself is reviewed, designers will need to build their data following input rules in their software. For that reason, MLIT is also developing creation standards that set the input rules for IFC data. Whether small and midsize design firms, not just the large ones, can keep up will be the key to wider adoption.

180 of Japan's tall chimneys, about 40%, show damage after a fatal collapse in Kumamoto

After a factory chimney collapsed in the Kumamoto earthquake, Japan's construction ministry surveyed 444 tall chimneys nationwide and found cracks and other problems in 180 of them, about 40%.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) on October 23 released the results of a survey of reinforced concrete chimneys taller than 60 m (197 ft) across Japan. The survey followed the collapse of a chimney at Nippon Paper Industries' Yatsushiro mill in Yatsushiro, Kumamoto Prefecture, on the southern island of Kyushu, in the Kumamoto earthquake of July 28 this year. Damage or deterioration was found in 180 of the 444 chimneys, about 40%.

The problems found included cracks, exposed rebar and corrosion. MLIT said the findings include minor cases and that it cannot conclude the chimneys are immediately unsafe. However, for 60 chimneys whose operators said they had no plans to take action, MLIT and other relevant ministries asked for emergency repairs.

Unlike buildings, chimneys are not covered by the system that requires owners to regularly report their condition and repairs to prefectures and other authorities. MLIT's expert panel is examining the cause of the collapse and measures to prevent another one, and a review of the rules, including possible new requirements, is one of the issues on the table.

Briefing
  • The survey was conducted from August 18 to September 7 through the relevant ministries. By prefecture, Hokkaido had the most with 21, followed by Hyogo with 20 and Yamaguchi with 16.
  • By industry, paper had the most with 36, followed by steel with 31 and cement with 29. They are concentrated in industries with many factories that have tall chimneys.
  • MLIT is also asking local governments and others to spread the word about emergency repair methods and to provide guidance to operators.
  • On September 10, Nippon Paper set up a damage investigation committee of outside experts. Chaired by Taiji Matsuda, professor emeritus at Kyushu University, it is examining how the chimney was damaged and collapsed, as well as safety measures for running the mill.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

I mentioned this on the show, too: under the law, a chimney isn't treated as a building. It's classed as a kosakubutsu, a structure, meaning something people don't live or work inside. So it was never measured with the same yardstick as buildings. But in Yatsushiro, nine people died in the office that stood where the chimney fell. Even with nobody inside, if there are people where it falls, lives are at stake. I see this survey as the first inspection that applies that fact to the whole country. You've probably seen the base of an old balcony railing rusted brown, haven't you? Cracks and exposed rebar are the same kind of thing: signs you can see from the outside. If you can see them, you can do something about them. The Building Standards Act is a law that has been rewritten after every major accident. This number, 180 chimneys, should lead to a system of inspections and reporting. As an architect, I want to see that through.

Background
Buildings versus structures

Buildings taller than 60 m (197 ft) must undergo time-history response analysis, a detailed simulation of how the building moves in an earthquake, and receive certification from the minister of MLIT. Chimneys, on the other hand, are handled under a separate framework as structures and are not subject to periodic reporting the way buildings are. The survey results give the debate over how to close that gap something to work with.

Findings due this fiscal year

As we reported on Sep 29, a national expert committee plans to compile its findings on the chimney collapse within fiscal 2026, together with the damage to the base-isolated Yatsushiro City Hall building. Alongside pinning down the cause, the next focus will be how to inspect existing chimneys across the country.

Osaka Castle's 1931 concrete keep to become an Important Cultural Property, a first for a reconstruction

A national council has recommended designating the main keep of Osaka Castle as an Important Cultural Property. It is the first time a castle keep reconstructed in the Showa era would receive the designation.

Japan's Council for Cultural Affairs recommended on October 16 that the Minister of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology designate the main keep of Osaka Castle as an Important Cultural Property, Japan's main national heritage designation for buildings and objects. The designation becomes official when it is published in the government's Official Gazette. According to the Agency for Cultural Affairs, it is the first time a keep built in the Showa era based on historical records and conjecture has been designated an Important Cultural Property.

The current keep was rebuilt in 1931 (Showa 6) in steel-framed reinforced concrete, funded by donations from the public, with the aim of recreating the keep from the era of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the warlord who built the original castle. It was recognized for its high value as a pioneer of how castle keeps are reconstructed, and for having been built as a museum and observation deck open to the public.

The Osaka Castle main keep became a national Registered Tangible Cultural Property in 1997, and this is an upgrade from that listing to the higher rank of Important Cultural Property. The same recommendation also called for designating the Dharma Hall of Kennin-ji temple in Kyoto and the main building of the Nara Hotel, among others, as Important Cultural Properties.

Briefing
  • The eight structures recommended include the Dharma Hall of Kennin-ji in Kyoto and the main building of the Nara Hotel. Important Cultural Properties are formally designated once the council's recommendation is published in the Official Gazette.
  • The director of the Osaka Castle keep has welcomed the designation as “a chance for people to learn about its value.” The keep is still open to the public as a museum.
  • On the other hand, Toshi Kahara, a historical commentator well versed in castle history, has questioned the reasons for the designation, since the keep is a reconstruction that includes parts that differ from the historical record. Opinions on how to judge it are divided.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

A reinforced concrete castle is becoming a national Important Cultural Property. Some of you may be thinking a castle keep that isn't made of wood is just a fake. And in fact, some castle experts have raised doubts. But what was recognized here isn't the Toyotomi-era keep itself. Ninety-five years ago, the people of Showa-era Osaka pooled their donations and built it with steel-framed reinforced concrete, the latest technology of the day. What was weighed was the value of that building in its own right. Preserving an old wooden keep and preserving a piece of Showa architecture are judged with different yardsticks, aren't they? Today's keep is less a copy of the original keep and more a work of its own time, 1931. I think it sits on the same line as Tokyo Tower and Showa-era buildings becoming cultural properties. Is a castle's value decided by the era it was built in, or by who built it and how? Which do you think it is?

Background
Modern buildings as heritage

Important Cultural Properties now include not only temples, shrines and castles but also modern buildings. Reinforced concrete and steel buildings are valued as records of the technology and society of the time they were built. As with the conversion of the former Yokohama City Hall by architect Togo Murano, which we reported on Oct 16, how to keep and use Showa-era architecture has become a shared theme across Japan.

Reconstructed keeps around Japan

Many castle keeps across Japan were rebuilt in reinforced concrete before and after World War II, and in some cases, such as Nagoya Castle, plans are moving ahead to rebuild them in wood. Many keeps also face challenges with earthquake resistance and accessibility, and local governments differ on whether to keep them as they are or go back to wood.
