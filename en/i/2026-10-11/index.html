Japan's housing ministry launches panel on a market where well-kept homes get fair prices

On Oct 8, Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) held the first meeting of a study group on housing maintenance and resale. Against the backdrop of soaring new-home prices, it will discuss market mechanisms that properly value high-quality existing homes.

Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) held the first meeting of its Study Group on Systems for the Maintenance and Distribution of Housing on Oct 8 in Toranomon, Tokyo. According to the meeting notice issued on Oct 2, the theme was "thinking about the housing maintenance that consumers want." The group will discuss a market framework that encourages owners to maintain the quality and performance of their homes, and ways to create an environment where buyers of existing homes can purchase with confidence. The meeting was closed to the public, and the agenda consisted of a presentation on "the current situation, issues and direction of study" followed by an exchange of views.

According to Housing Tribune (Housing Today), the background is soaring prices for new homes and a decline in housing starts. The aim is to create a market in which high-quality existing homes are properly valued and can be bought, sold and passed on with confidence. At the first meeting, the ministry laid out a direction for building a framework in which the market properly evaluates a building's quality and performance and how well it has been maintained.

The same week, on Oct 7, MLIT also launched a study group to review its guidelines for preparing long-term repair plans, the schedules Japanese condominium associations use to plan major repairs. On both fronts, condominium repairs and the maintenance of housing including detached houses, the government has begun at the same time to rethink, at the level of policy, how homes are cared for after they're built and how that care is recognized as value.

Briefing
  • The first meeting was held on Oct 8 from 10 a.m. to noon in a meeting room in Toranomon. It was closed to the public, with cameras allowed only at the opening (MLIT meeting notice)
  • On Oct 7 of the same week, the first meeting of the Study Group on Revising the Guidelines for Preparing Long-Term Repair Plans was also held, discussing revisions to condominium repair plans and repair reserve funds
  • MLIT estimates put the average age of demolished homes at 32.1 years in Japan, 66.6 years in the US and 80.6 years in the UK (cited by Sho, Architect on his Oct 5 show)

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

The average age of homes when they're demolished is 32 years in Japan, 67 years in the US and 81 years in the UK. Those are MLIT estimates I mentioned on my show. I don't think Japanese homes are short-lived just because they're built of wood. I think it's because, as homes turned from "places to live" into "assets," a culture of repairing old houses and continuing to use them never took root. What's interesting about this study group is that it tackles head-on the question of how the market should value the fact that a home has been fixed. Picture two houses, both 30 years old. One has had insulation upgrades, a new roof and repaired exterior walls, with records kept. The other has had nothing done. In today's resale market, those two can sell for almost the same price. So there's no incentive to take care of a home. Even on a small extension project I'm involved in right now, the first step was checking with the evaluation agency and the ward office whether the house could keep its Long-Life Quality Housing certification, a national label for durable, well-maintained homes. The path for maintenance records to become value within the system is still narrow. Does your parents' house have a record of the repairs it's had? From a competition to build to a competition to keep using. I think that's the era we've entered.

Background
How existing homes are valued

In Japan, the custom of depreciating a building's value uniformly by its age has been strong, and wooden detached houses have often been appraised as having almost no building value after 20 to 25 years. Since the 2010s, MLIT has put in place systems such as performance evaluations for existing homes, inspections (building condition surveys) and renovation programs to bring homes up to Long-Life Quality Housing standards, but mechanisms that carry through to market prices are still limited.

The backdrop of soaring new-home prices

Rising construction costs and land prices have made new homes harder to buy, and the focus of policy is shifting toward making use of existing homes. MLIT is also moving ahead with low-interest loans for operators that renovate vacant houses to rent out (planned to start in fiscal 2027) and with more favorable mortgage tax breaks for existing homes. This study group's role is to create the yardstick for valuing them.

Nagoya's new Kanayama theater to open in fiscal 2035 via PFI, with 2,100- and 1,400-seat halls for ¥58 billion

On Oct 1, Nagoya released the implementation policy and draft performance requirements for a new theater replacing its civic hall in Kanayama. Built under a BTO-type PFI, the project will be put out to tender in April 2027, aiming to open in fiscal 2035.

On Oct 1, the city of Nagoya released the implementation policy and draft performance requirements for its new theater development and operation project, which will replace the Nagoya Civic Hall in Kanayama, a major transit hub south of central Nagoya. The hall is currently known as Nihon Tokushu Toryo Shimin Kaikan under a naming-rights deal. According to the Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, a construction industry newspaper, the project will use a BTO-type PFI (private finance initiative), in which a private company designs and builds the facility, hands it over to the city and also runs it. The city plans to announce an open tender covered by the WTO Agreement on Government Procurement in April 2027, accept bids and proposals in September and choose the winner in December. A site tour will be held on Nov 9.

The new theater will have a total floor area of about 30,900 m² (333,000 sq ft). It will include Hall 1, with 2,100 or more seats and designed mainly for music, and Hall 2, with 1,400 or more seats and designed mainly for theater, dance and traditional performing arts, along with studios, rehearsal rooms and a shared lobby. Redevelopment of the adjacent Furusawa Park is also part of the project. Design and construction will take about seven years from fiscal 2028, with the goal of opening in fiscal 2035. The estimated project cost is about ¥58 billion.

The current civic hall is scheduled to close at the end of fiscal 2027. According to the Chunichi Shimbun, Nagoya will have fewer large halls in the period until the new theater opens in fiscal 2035. Next door, the Asunal Kanayama shopping complex is also set to be demolished by February 2028, with plans moving forward for a new high-rise mixed-use complex.

Briefing
  • Schedule: tender announced April 2027, bids and proposals accepted in September, winner chosen in December, about seven years of design and construction from fiscal 2028, and opening in fiscal 2035 (Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun)
  • The draft basic plan of October 2024 projected about 2,200 seats for Hall 1 and about 1,500 for Hall 2. The draft performance requirements call for 2,100 or more and 1,400 or more
  • The current civic hall is scheduled to close at the end of fiscal 2027. The adjacent Asunal Kanayama is to be demolished by February 2028, with plans to develop a mixed-use complex jointly with private partners (Tokai TV)

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Two thousand one hundred seats and 1,400 seats. These two numbers are the very first ones decided in designing a concert hall. Once the seat count is set, everything else follows in a chain: the width and depth of the stage, the rake of the seating, the size of the lobby, even how many restrooms you need. So the fact that the basic plan's figures of about 2,200 and about 1,500 seats have dropped to "2,100 or more" and "1,400 or more" in these requirements may look like a small change, but it touches the skeleton of the building. Just like the new MICE (meetings, incentives, conventions and exhibitions) facility in Sapporo we covered on Oct 9, a hall needs a large column-free space, so its construction cost per square meter is unavoidably high. Divide ¥58 billion by 30,900 m² (333,000 sq ft) and you get about ¥1.9 million per square meter. Given today's construction costs, I understand the city's decision to hold down the project cost even if it means a few fewer seats. But a hall is a building you use for 50 years once it's built. Are 2,100 and 1,400 seats the right size for Nagoya in 2035? And where in the city would you look for the information to judge that?

Background
The BTO model

BTO stands for Build-Transfer-Operate. It is a type of PFI in which a private company builds a facility, transfers ownership to the local government immediately on completion, and then handles maintenance and operation over a long period. It differs from the BT plus concession model used for Sapporo's new MICE facility in that it does not involve selling the operating rights.

How the rebuild came about

Nagoya Civic Hall opened in 1972. At first there was a plan to extend its life through renovation, but the rebuilding plan was revised in 2021, and it was reported that there would be a five-year period without the hall. The city drew up a basic concept in 2022 and a basic plan in February 2025, and with this release of the implementation policy, the PFI process is now fully underway.

Shinjuku drafts plan for 34-story, 140 m redevelopment in Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome South, aiming for 2032

Tokyo's Shinjuku Ward has drafted an urban plan for the Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome South redevelopment on about 0.6 hectares (1.5 acres) along Juniso-dori avenue. The 34-story, roughly 40,600 m² (437,000 sq ft) tower aims for an urban planning decision in February 2027 and completion in fiscal 2032.

Shinjuku Ward in Tokyo has put together a draft urban plan for the Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome South District Category 1 Urban Redevelopment Project, which covers about 0.6 hectares (1.5 acres) along Juniso-dori avenue. This type of project is a Japanese scheme in which landowners pool their plots to build a larger complex. The Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun, a construction industry newspaper, reported on Oct 8 that after public comments and resident briefings, the plan will be reported to the ward's urban planning council in November and turned into a formal draft. The ward will put it on public display and accept written opinions in January 2027, aiming for an urban planning decision in February that year.

The redevelopment building will be a 34-story reinforced concrete tower with a total floor area of about 40,600 m² (437,000 sq ft) and a height of about 140 m (460 ft). Retail and parking will occupy the lower floors and housing the middle and upper floors, with two plazas on the north side. The site is about 300 m (980 ft) northeast of Nishi-Shinjuku-gochome Station on the Toei Oedo subway line and about 1.2 km (0.75 miles) northwest of JR Shinjuku Station. The preparatory association was formed in 2016, and Sumitomo Realty & Development is the project partner.

Going forward, the plan calls for the redevelopment association to be established in fiscal 2027; approval of the rights conversion plan, the step that swaps landowners' existing property for space in the new building, and demolition of existing buildings in fiscal 2028; start of construction in fiscal 2029; and completion and move-in in fiscal 2032. In Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome, redevelopments involving Sumitomo Realty are also underway in the neighboring Central South and North districts, further pushing high-rise development along Juniso-dori.

Briefing
  • Schedule: urban planning decision within fiscal 2026 (February 2027), association established in fiscal 2027, rights conversion approval and demolition in fiscal 2028, construction start in fiscal 2029, and completion and move-in in fiscal 2032 (Kensetsu Tsushin Shimbun)
  • Uses are housing, retail and parking. The middle and upper floors are housing, making it mainly a tower condominium. Two plazas will be built on the north side
  • Redevelopment is also underway in the adjacent Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome Central South District (association-led), and twin towers about 151 m (495 ft) tall are planned for the Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome North District under a disaster-prevention block improvement project, a scheme for rebuilding fire-prone neighborhoods

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

About 1.2 km (0.75 miles) west of Shinjuku Station. Juniso-dori is a kind of borderline in Shinjuku, a street where the skyscraper office district sits right next to a dense neighborhood of wooden houses. Over the past 10 years, redevelopments involving Sumitomo Realty have been moving along this street from north to south. This South district covers 0.6 hectares (1.5 acres) and gets a 34-story tower. By area alone it's small, but what I'm watching is the "two plazas on the north side." In redevelopments of dense wooden-house neighborhoods, the real goal, even more than building a tower, is to create roads that fire trucks can get into and open space where people can take refuge in a fire. The 140 m (460 ft) building is, in a sense, the price paid to create those plazas. The preparatory association for this district was formed in 2016. That means 11 years to the urban planning decision and 16 years to completion. Redevelopment is work where the time spent building agreement among people is far longer than the time spent building the building. In Japan, you often see a redevelopment "preparatory association" sign posted in a neighborhood long before anything gets built. If there's one in your neighborhood, how long has it been up? The target for completion and move-in is fiscal 2032.

Background
Nishi-Shinjuku 5-chome's redevelopments

The area around Nishi-Shinjuku-gochome Station on the Toei Oedo Line is a dense wooden-house district spreading west of the Shinjuku skyscraper district. Since the 2010s, several urban redevelopment and disaster-prevention block improvement projects, including the North, Central South and South districts, have moved forward in stages. Sumitomo Realty & Development serves as project partner or participating member in many of them, and Juniso-dori is turning into a street of high-rise housing.

From draft plan to groundbreaking

An association-led urban redevelopment project in Japan goes through a set sequence: urban planning decision, approval of the association, approval of the rights conversion plan, then demolition and construction. Each stage requires landowner consent, and it is not unusual for two to three years to pass from the urban planning decision to groundbreaking, or more than 10 years counting from the formation of the preparatory association.
