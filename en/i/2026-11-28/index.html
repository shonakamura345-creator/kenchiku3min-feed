Kobe's Japan Pearl Hall, a 1952 registered heritage building, is replaced by a 13-story condo and pearl center

Tokyo Tatemono and the Japan Pearl Exporters' Association announced the completion of the rebuilt Japan Pearl Hall in Kobe's Former Foreign Settlement. A new pearl hall fills the first two floors, with 53 condominiums above.

Developer Tokyo Tatemono and the Japan Pearl Exporters' Association announced on Nov 26 that Brillia Kobe Kyu-Kyoryuchi, a mixed-use condominium building, has been completed as part of the project to rebuild the Japan Pearl Hall. The site is in the Former Foreign Settlement (Kyu-Kyoryuchi) of Kobe's Chuo Ward, a historic district in the port city of Kobe, west of Osaka. The reinforced concrete building has 13 floors above ground and one below. It was completed on Oct 5, and handover of the homes is scheduled to begin in mid-December.

The old hall was built in 1952 and served as the hub of Japan's pearl industry for about 70 years. It became a nationally Registered Tangible Cultural Property in 2005, but closed in March 2023 because of its age. On the first and second floors of the new building, the association has set up a new Japan Pearl Hall, with offices, a pearl auction room and a museum. The Kobe Pearl Museum, closed since then, is set to reopen as the JAPAN PEARL MUSEUM in April 2027.

The upper floors hold 53 condominiums for sale, the first new condo development in the Former Foreign Settlement in 14 years. The exterior borrows the vertical lines and the three-part composition of base, middle and top seen in the district's Western-style buildings. The balcony railings are modeled on the prongs that hold a pearl in place.

Briefing
  • The building was designed by D&D Architects, built by Ohki Komuten, with design supervision by Jun Mitsui & Associates Architects. Tokyo Tatemono is the seller and building owner, and it ran the project together with the Japan Pearl Exporters' Association, which owned the old building.
  • When construction start was announced in July 2024, completion was scheduled for late July 2026. The building was actually completed on Oct 5, and the announcement came about a month and a half later, on Nov 26.
  • The old hall is said to have been designed by Yoshimitsu Mitsuyasu, who worked in Hyogo Prefecture's building department, and it was valued as a modernist building symbolic of Kobe. The homes range from about 38.7 to 147.7 m² (417 to 1,590 sq ft).

The prongs that hold a pearl. That's the motif for the balcony railings on this new condo building. Think of a pearl ring, where tiny prongs on the setting grip the pearl from four sides. That shape. The Japan Pearl Hall, built in 1952, was where pearls were traded for about 70 years. It was also a nationally registered cultural property. But here's the thing: registration doesn't require anyone to keep the building. With a notification, the owner can choose to rebuild. What was chosen here was a new building that keeps the work and the place. The pearl auction room and the museum are coming back to the first and second floors, on the same site. Instead of the association rebuilding on its own, it teamed up with a developer and built it together with housing. That's a common way old buildings get rebuilt in city centers, right? It is sad to lose a fine modernist building. Even so, the sign of the pearl town stayed at the same address. The museum reopens in April 2027.

Background
Limits of registration

Japan's Registered Tangible Cultural Property status is much looser than a designation such as Important Cultural Property. Major exterior changes or even demolition only require a notification. The system aims to spread registration by keeping the burden on owners light, but it does not guarantee that a building survives.

The Former Foreign Settlement

Kobe's Former Foreign Settlement is a district laid out in the Meiji era, the late 19th century, as a residential area for foreigners. Modern Western-style buildings remain there, while many sites have been rebuilt as offices and shops. New housing is rare, and this is the first condo development there in 14 years.

Kengo Kuma and five other creators show off MTG PARK in Nagoya's Atsuta district, opening spring 2027

Beauty device maker MTG opened its MTG PARK headquarters complex, under construction near Atsuta Shrine in Nagoya, to the press. Six creators including Kengo Kuma are involved, with an opening planned for spring 2027.

MTG, known for beauty device brands such as ReFa, held a site tour on Nov 24 at MTG PARK, a complex under construction in Nagoya's Atsuta Ward, and a press presentation the same day at the Atsuta Jingu Hall. Atsuta Shrine is one of the best-known Shinto shrines in Nagoya, the central Japanese city between Tokyo and Osaka. The complex is scheduled to open in spring 2027.

MTG PARK will house MTG's new headquarters along with a Japanese restaurant, other restaurants and a cafe. It is positioned as one part of the Atsuta Gaien Project, an effort to bring more activity to the area around Atsuta Shrine. Kengo Kuma & Associates is responsible for the design and supervision of the whole complex.

At the presentation, six people were introduced as the creators: architect Kengo Kuma, sukiya builder Koji Nakamura, spatial designers Ryu Kosaka and Keita Aono, flower artist HIKARU SEINO and artist Rocco Ritchie. Sukiya is a traditional Japanese style of refined wooden architecture associated with tea houses. On the site tour, Kuma showed visitors the lobby under construction and explained its design, with walls that undulate in three dimensions.

Briefing
  • According to MTG's official announcement, the complex will include an R&D center and a brand museum in addition to the headquarters. The cafe and restaurants are planned as spaces the general public can use.
  • The Japanese restaurant is a kaiseki restaurant called Roketsu, with the involvement of Nakamura Sotoji Komuten, a builder known for sukiya architecture. After the site tour, a press presentation was held at the Atsuta Jingu Hall.
  • The exact opening date and the total project cost were not disclosed in this announcement.

Background
Headquarters that open up

Corporate headquarters used to be buildings only employees went in and out of. In recent years, more headquarters put cafes or exhibitions on the ground floor to draw in local people. The idea is to give the building itself a role as a place to experience the brand.

Atsuta as a place

In Nagoya, retail is being reshuffled, including PARCO_ya Nagoya in the Sakae district, which we reported on Nov 24. Unlike the Meieki station area or Sakae, Atsuta is a district centered on Atsuta Shrine. The Atsuta Gaien Project aims to bring activity to the area around the shrine, and MTG PARK is one part of it.

Penta-Ocean and Ohmoto Gumi suspended from bidding for 6 weeks over off-plan work at Kitakyushu Airport

The Kyushu Regional Development Bureau suspended general contractors Penta-Ocean Construction and Ohmoto Gumi from its bidding for six weeks after work on a site preparation project at Kitakyushu Airport was found to differ from the design documents.

On Nov 16, the Kyushu Regional Development Bureau of the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) suspended Penta-Ocean Construction and Ohmoto Gumi from bidding for six weeks. The reason: work that differed from the design documents on a project to prepare land for the runway extension at Kitakyushu Airport in Kanda, Fukuoka Prefecture, in southwestern Japan. The suspension runs until Dec 27, and during that time the two companies cannot bid on projects ordered by the bureau.

The work was contracted to a joint venture (JV) of the two companies. Without consulting the client in advance, the JV allegedly carried out work that differed from the design documents on groundwater measures for the drainage, the connecting pipes and the lighting facilities.

The problem came to light after the work was finished, when another contractor dug up the ground to build a side ditch. Drainage pipes were found buried shallower than the design documents specified. Later investigation also found errors in the positions of the lighting foundations and in the angles of pipes connecting to manholes, according to reports.

Briefing
  • The project was the fiscal 2024 Kitakyushu Airport runway extension site preparation work. According to Nikkei xTECH, the same JV carried out the work in fiscal 2024 and 2025.
  • Kitakyushu Airport is in the middle of a project to extend its runway. This work prepared the land to be used for that extension.
  • A bidding suspension is different from a criminal penalty or a business suspension under Japan's Construction Business Act. It is a measure in which a client bars a company from its bidding for a set period, and it does not directly affect projects ordered by other clients.

A drainage pipe buried shallower than the drawings said. It was found by a different company digging a side ditch after the work was done. Once a pipe is in the ground, nobody sees it again. That's exactly why the rules say that if you change something from the drawings, you consult the client and keep a record. At home, it's like rerouting the electrical wiring behind the wallpaper without telling the owner. It looks the same, but the next person who drills into the wall is in trouble, right? The reports so far don't explain why the changes were made. Every site has reasons things can't go exactly by the drawings. What's being questioned is less the change itself than skipping the consultation step. The more a site is pressed for time, the more paperwork gets pushed back. Even when it's a hassle, you stop once and check with the client. I think that habit, repeated, is what protects the quality you can't see underground.

Background
Work you can't see

Parts like drainage pipes and foundations end up hidden underground once a project is finished, which makes them hard to check later. That's why public works in Japan use stage inspections, recording the condition with photos or on-site witnesses before backfilling. In this case, the discrepancy was found only because another project dug the ground up again.

Site changes and consultation

On site, it's not unusual for ground or groundwater conditions to differ from expectations, making it hard to build exactly to the drawings. The rule in that case is to consult the client and change the design before building. What's in question here is that the changes were made without that consultation.
