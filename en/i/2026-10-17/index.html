Kyoto to raise height limit south of Kyoto Station from 31 m to 45 m amid office shortage

Kyoto City has decided to raise the height limit for buildings south of Kyoto Station from the current 31 m (102 ft) to 45 m (148 ft). A shortage of office space near the station is behind the move.

Kyoto City has decided to ease the rules that cap building heights around Kyoto Station, starting with the area south of the station. According to reports by public broadcaster NHK and the Kyoto Shimbun newspaper on October 16, the maximum height there, currently 31 m (102 ft), would be raised to 45 m (148 ft).

The city says that on the south side of the station, public spaces are being improved and companies from outside the city are moving in, building momentum for redevelopment. Behind the move are a shortage of office space in front of Kyoto Station and aging buildings. According to the Nikkei business newspaper, Mayor Koji Matsui had indicated that, out of concern for the cityscape, he would move first on easing the rules for the south side.

How the north side of the station will be treated was not made clear in these reports. On the north side, there is a plan to rebuild Kyoto Central Post Office as a mixed-use building with offices, shops and a bus terminal, and the Kyoto Shimbun reports that the developer is aiming, through a relaxation of the rules, for a height of about 60 m (197 ft), the same as the Kyoto Station Building.

Briefing
  • In a revision of its urban planning rules that took effect in 2023, Kyoto City had already raised the height limit along major streets on the south side of Kyoto Station from 20 to 25 m (66–82 ft) to 31 m (102 ft). The new 45 m (148 ft) plan is a further relaxation following that.
  • The Kyoto Chamber of Commerce and Industry had earlier submitted a written request to Mayor Matsui asking that the height limit in front of Kyoto Station be eased from 31 m (102 ft) to 60 m (197 ft).
  • The Kyoto Shimbun cites attracting companies and preventing talent from leaving the city as the aims of the new policy, and reports that interest in development on the south side of the station is already rising.

31 meters versus 45 meters. The difference is 14 m (46 ft), roughly three to four floors of an office building. Kyoto is an unusual city that puts a height cap on buildings across almost its entire urban area. The rule is there so that the ridgelines of the Higashiyama and Kitayama mountains, the hills along the city's east and north, can be seen from anywhere in town. Think of a height limit as making everyone in a movie theater sit at the same height. If one person is much taller, the people behind them can't see the screen, right? But by evening out everyone's height so thoroughly, the city ran out of room to build new offices in front of the station. People may want to work in Kyoto, but there's nowhere to work. That's the starting point for this relaxation. The city will move on the south side first. On the north side, some are hoping for 60 meters, the same as the station building. Protecting the view, and creating places to work. Where's the right place to draw the line? What do you think?

Background
Kyoto's New Landscape Policy

In 2007, Kyoto City launched its New Landscape Policy and lowered its building height limits. The maximum, which had been 45 m (148 ft), was cut to 31 m (102 ft), and in historic districts to as low as 15 m (49 ft). The new 45 m limit can be seen as returning the area south of the station, and only that area, to the level before this policy.

Protect here, develop there

Historic cities across Japan are increasingly dividing themselves into areas that protect the streetscape and areas that absorb economic activity, and setting rules of different strictness for each. Kyoto City, too, has been revising its rules to keep protecting the areas that are key to its cityscape while easing restrictions in some areas, such as south of the station and the eastern part of the city.

ANA to move its head office to YAESU CORE, a 227 m tower in front of Tokyo Station, in spring 2030

ANA Holdings and All Nippon Airways announced on Oct 16 that they will move their head office from Shiodome to YAESU CORE, a redevelopment tower in Tokyo's Yaesu district in front of Tokyo Station, around spring 2030.

ANA Holdings and All Nippon Airways announced on October 16 that they will move their head office functions from their current home in Shiodome City Center, in the Shiodome business district near Shinbashi, to YAESU CORE, a redevelopment tower under construction in Yaesu 2-chome, on the east side of Tokyo Station. The move is planned for around spring 2030. Some of the group's functions will move at the same time to a new base by the west exit of Tamachi Station, also in central Tokyo.

YAESU CORE is being built under the Yaesu 2-chome Central District Type 1 Urban Redevelopment Project, part of a Japanese scheme in which landowners pool their plots to build a larger complex. The mixed-use tower will have 43 floors above ground and three below, and stand about 227 m (745 ft) tall. Along with offices, it will house retail, a theater with about 1,300 seats and a highway bus terminal, and is scheduled for completion at the end of January 2029. Nikken Sekkei did the basic design, and Kajima Corporation is handling detailed design and construction.

ANA says the aim of the move is to create a better working environment for employees. Which companies and departments will go to Yaesu and which to Tamachi will be decided later.

Briefing
  • YAESU CORE's theater will occupy the 3rd to 6th floors. Hankyu Corporation is planning the facility and Umeda Arts Theater will run it. Musicals, the Takarazuka Revue (the all-female musical theater company) and concerts are planned.
  • The underground Bus Terminal Tokyo Yaesu will operate as a single facility spanning three developments: Tokyo Midtown Yaesu, TOFROM YAESU and YAESU CORE. YAESU CORE will add seven boarding and alighting bays.
  • The redevelopment is being carried out by the Yaesu 2-chome Central District Urban Redevelopment Association, with Kajima Corporation, Sumitomo Realty & Development and the Urban Renaissance Agency (UR), a government-affiliated urban development body, involved. The name YAESU CORE was announced on August 24, 2026.

Background
Three Yaesu redevelopments

On the Yaesu side of Tokyo Station, three redevelopments have been moving forward in parallel: the Yaesu 1-chome East District, the Yaesu 2-chome North District and the Yaesu 2-chome Central District. YAESU CORE, in the Central District, is billed as the culmination of the three redevelopments in front of Tokyo Station, and the underground bus terminal will join all three into one. Another goal is to consolidate highway bus stops that had been scattered along the streets.

Head offices and redevelopment

For a large redevelopment tower under construction, a major company moving its head office in means a big office tenant is secured before completion. In central Tokyo, large office buildings keep being completed, and companies are increasingly gathering in buildings that sit on top of transportation hubs.

Shimizu invests in US humanoid robot startup Noble Machines, plans site trials from spring 2027

Shimizu Corporation has announced an investment in Noble Machines, a US startup developing AI-powered humanoid robots for construction sites. Testing on real sites is set to begin in spring 2027.

Shimizu Corporation, one of Japan's five largest general contractors, has announced that it has completed an investment in Noble Machines, a Silicon Valley startup developing AI-powered humanoid robots for the construction industry. The Nikkei business newspaper reported the news on October 16. The amount of the investment has not been disclosed.

Noble Machines was founded in 2024. It develops robot hardware designed to work in harsh environments such as construction sites, along with the control systems and operating software. Shimizu says it will bring its construction know-how to the development work, and the two companies are starting their collaboration in earnest.

By the end of 2026, the companies plan to test moving materials and getting around and patrolling a site in a virtual space built from 3D building models. From spring 2027, they plan to move on to trials with actual robots on real construction sites.

Briefing
  • In July 2026, Shimizu announced it was ramping up research and development toward putting AI robots to work on construction sites, and began trials of site patrols by humanoid robots and painting by robotic arms.
  • Around the same time, Shimizu also invested in MESH AG, a Swiss company aiming to automate rebar work using physical AI, and it continues to invest in overseas robotics startups.
  • Noble Machines' robots include the MOBY series of humanoids for the construction industry.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Testing begins in spring 2027. Humanoid robots will be walking around actual construction sites. Why go to the trouble of making them human-shaped? It seems strange, doesn't it? In a factory, you can just put an arm-only robot next to a conveyor belt. But a construction site is a place whose shape changes every day. Where there was a floor yesterday, a wall stands today. Stairs are temporary, and materials are lying around underfoot. For a robot on wheels, it's like driving through a room on moving day, crammed with cardboard boxes. The thinking is that in a place built for people to walk through, a robot shaped like a person can get around most easily. They'll start by testing jobs like carrying materials and patrolling the site. It's less a replacement for skilled tradespeople than a helper that gives them more time to do the work that needs their skills. For sites short on workers, starting from that kind of role seems like the realistic way in.

Background
General contractors and robots

Japan's major general contractors have so far developed dedicated robots for individual tasks, such as welding or moving materials. Recently, with advances in AI, attention has turned to general-purpose humanoid robots that can handle several tasks with one machine, and more contractors are teaming up with overseas startups.

The reality of labor shortages

Japan's construction industry faces an aging skilled workforce and a shortage of young workers, and since April 2024 legal caps on overtime have also applied to the industry. To meet construction schedules with limited staff, companies are moving to hand off tasks people don't need to do themselves, such as carrying materials and patrolling sites, to robots.
