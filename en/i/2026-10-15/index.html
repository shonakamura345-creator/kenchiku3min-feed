九州電力（公式）

Rebuilding begins on the former Palace Hotel Hakone site, aiming for a 69-room reopening in fall 2029

A groundbreaking ceremony was held on October 13 at the site of Palace Hotel Hakone, which opened in 1960. The new four-story resort with one basement level and 69 rooms is set to open in fall 2029.

Palace Hotel Co., Kyushu Electric Power and Craftia announced on October 14 that construction of a replacement hotel has started on the site of the former Palace Hotel Hakone in Sengokuhara, a highland district of Hakone, the hot-spring resort area in the mountains southwest of Tokyo in Kanagawa Prefecture. A groundbreaking ceremony was held the day before, on October 13. The new hotel will carry on the Palace Hotel Hakone name and is scheduled to open in fall 2029.

The original hotel was licensed as a lodging business inside a national park and opened in 1960 under its earlier name, Hakone Kanko Hotel. It was a well-loved fixture for 56 years but closed in January 2018 because the building had aged. The new hotel is planned to open about 11 years after the closure.

The new building will have four floors above ground and one basement level, with a total floor area of about 12,850 m² (138,000 sq ft). It will have 69 rooms in all, including six suites and five villas. Plans call for three restaurants and bars, including a restaurant and a lounge bar, along with a spa, a pool and a fitness gym.

Briefing
  • The address is 1245 Sengokuhara, Hakone Town, Ashigarashimo District, Kanagawa Prefecture. Media reports describe it as a resort hotel inside a national park with views of Mount Fuji.
  • The three dining venues are an all-day dining restaurant, a lounge bar and a specialty restaurant. Wellness facilities include a spa, a pool and a fitness gym.
  • The project is being carried out by three companies: Palace Hotel, Kyushu Electric Power and Craftia. Kyushu Electric Power also announced the October 13 groundbreaking and the start of construction in its own press release.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Four floors above ground, 69 rooms. For a total floor area of 12,850 m² (138,000 sq ft), that means laying the rooms out generously in a low building. Hotels in the middle of a big city keep reaching higher and higher, right? This is the opposite. The setting is inside a national park. In national parks, buildings have to respect the landscape, down to their height, color and shape. If it were a bath, the size of the tub would be fixed from the start. Within that frame, they held the count to 69 rooms, six suites and five villas included. I read that as a decision to compete on the space each room gets and the nature around it, not on the number of rooms. Having a spa and pool inside the hotel also points to a stay-put design, where you can spend the whole day without heading out. The old hotel opened in 1960 and ran here in Sengokuhara for 56 years. It will be 11 years from closing to reopening. In fall 2029, a new light will come on in Sengokuhara, where the fields of pampas grass spread out.

Background
Hotels inside national parks

Inside Japan's national parks, the Natural Parks Act limits the height, color, size and other aspects of buildings. Lodging can be run as a government-approved “park project,” and the original Palace Hotel Hakone opened under this kind of license for lodging. Balancing the landscape against the size of the building is a basic starting condition for the design.

東京商工リサーチ（公式）

Labor-shortage bankruptcies in Japan hit a record 240 in April to September, 55 in construction

Tokyo Shoko Research counted a record 240 bankruptcies caused by labor shortages in the first half of fiscal 2026, 55 of them in construction. Construction bankruptcies for September alone, however, fell from a year earlier.

Tokyo Shoko Research, one of Japan's two major corporate credit research firms, announced in October that 240 companies went bankrupt because of labor shortages in the first half of fiscal 2026 (April to September), a new record. That is up 17.0% from the same period a year earlier. The count covers bankruptcies with liabilities of ¥10 million or more, and the causes include being unable to hire enough workers and being unable to absorb rising labor costs.

By industry, services and others accounted for 91 cases and construction for 55 (up 5.7% year on year), together making up 60% of the total. By cause, bankruptcies due to soaring labor costs numbered 130, more than half. By size, small companies with capital of less than ¥10 million accounted for 159 cases, nearly two-thirds of the total.

Construction bankruptcies for September alone, on the other hand, declined. Tokyo Shoko Research counted 132, down 27.4% from a year earlier, while Teikoku Databank, the other major credit research firm, counted 145, down 18.5%. The two firms use different criteria, so their numbers can't be compared directly, but both came in below the previous year.

Briefing
  • According to Tokyo Shoko Research, there were 831 corporate bankruptcies nationwide in September, down 4.8% from a year earlier. Construction was one of four industries below the previous year's level, and its count was the lowest so far this year.
  • The firm expects the rise in labor-shortage bankruptcies to continue, saying that minimum wage increases across Japan from October onward will deepen “wage-hike fatigue” caused by high prices and rising labor costs.
  • In Teikoku Databank's September report, bankruptcies among construction companies with short operating histories fell to 33 from 52 a year earlier.
  • According to Teikoku Databank, construction bankruptcies in the first half of calendar 2026 (January to June) totaled 1,043, up 5.8% from a year earlier. Even with some months showing declines, the yearly level remains high.

240. Over six months, 240 companies went under because they couldn't find enough people. I've talked about this on my show too: in regional Japan, there are companies that can't even bid on public works because they don't have the staff, even when the work is there. The work exists, but there are no hands to take it on. These numbers are an extension of that. What concerns me is that more than half were caused by soaring labor costs. Raising tradespeople's pay is something the industry has long wished for, right? But when the employer is a small company with less than ¥10 million in capital, it can't pass the higher labor costs fully on to its construction prices. It's like a window in winter: the bigger the gap between the warm room and the cold outside, the more condensation drips down the glass. The gap between the push to raise wages and the strength to pay them is seeping out in the form of bankruptcies. Construction bankruptcies did fall in September. Even so, exits from the industry, including closures, remain at a high level. Raising pay while keeping companies alive. Who ends up paying for that in the end?

Background
Caught between pay and survival

The national government has made higher pay for skilled workers a goal for the whole industry, and discussion of a shift to monthly salaries, which we reported on Oct 14, is moving ahead. But for the small specialty subcontractors that actually pay the wages, rising labor costs are a direct burden on the business. The question is how to raise workers' conditions while keeping the companies that support them healthy.

More closures than bankruptcies

According to Teikoku Databank, 4,894 construction companies suspended operations, closed or dissolved in the first half of 2026 (January to June), more than four times the number of bankruptcies. Combined with bankruptcies, these “exits” totaled 5,937, the most ever for a first half, surpassing 2009, just after the Lehman Brothers collapse. Bankruptcies are only one part of the trend of companies disappearing from the industry.

星野リゾート（公式・PR TIMES）

Hoshino Resorts opens KAI Zao in Yamagata, with a 360-degree rooftop terrace modeled on the Okama crater lake

Hoshino Resorts' hot-spring ryokan KAI Zao opens on October 15 in Zao Onsen, Yamagata. Architect Shuhei Kamiya supervised the design, using the Okama crater lake as the motif for the rooftop terrace.

Hoshino Resorts opens KAI Zao, a hot-spring ryokan (a traditional Japanese inn), on October 15 in Zao Onsen, a hot-spring town in the city of Yamagata in Yamagata Prefecture, northern Japan. It is the newest property in KAI, the company's nationwide brand of hot-spring ryokan, and Hoshino Resorts' first in Yamagata Prefecture. The concept, roughly “a hot-spring spiral that resets you at Okama,” pairs architecture inspired by Okama, the crater lake of the Zao mountain range, with the area's strongly acidic hot-spring water.

The building's main feature is a rooftop terrace with 360-degree views. A jacuzzi sits at the center of the terrace, which takes Okama as its motif, and the area around it, including a sauna and a lounge, forms a “loop-style wellness area” that guests can wander freely. The views are said to take in the Zao mountains to the east and farmland with the distant Asahi mountains to the west.

Kamiya Architects, led by architect Shuhei Kamiya, handled the basic architectural design and design supervision, Oryza did the landscape design, and Ichimura Komuten was the builder. All 49 guest rooms are “Kurenai Modern” rooms, the property's version of KAI's signature local-themed rooms, which bring in regional culture.

Briefing
  • Rates start at ¥31,000 per person for two people sharing a room, including tax, service charge, dinner and breakfast. Reservations opened on April 22, 2026.
  • Zao Onsen is known as one of Japan's leading strongly acidic sulfur hot springs. The large baths are reported to offer three tubs at different concentrations to suit the strong water.
  • Shuhei Kamiya took part as architect and creative director. In a piece written for the Hoshino Resorts website, he said he expressed Zao's culture and climate in the architecture through conversations with local craftspeople.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

After a hot-spring bath, where do you go to cool down? KAI Zao is a building that lifts that after-bath spot up to the roof. A jacuzzi sits in the middle of a terrace shaped after the Okama crater lake, and you can circle around through the sauna and lounge. True to the word spiral, the design turns simply walking through the building into part of the experience. In ryokan, the large shared baths are usually on floors close to the ground. Water is heavy and the plumbing gets complicated, so lower floors are easier to support. I think they still put the terrace on the roof because they wanted to layer the 360-degree view, the Zao mountains to the east and the Asahi mountains to the west, onto that after-bath time. One more thing: strongly acidic water is a tough opponent for a building. Metal deteriorates easily, and the choice of finish materials decides how long things last. Next time you visit a hot spring, take a moment to notice what the edges of the tubs and the handrails are made of.

Background
KAI's local-themed rooms

A hallmark of the KAI brand is that each property has local-themed rooms whose interiors draw on the culture of the area. At KAI Zao, all 49 rooms are “Kurenai Modern” rooms, expressing the region in the room finishes too. Expressing the place through both the building's form and its interiors is an idea shared across the brand.

Acidic springs and buildings

Strongly acidic hot-spring water easily corrodes metal, which makes the choice of piping and bathroom finishes a major design challenge. At hot-spring inns, damage to equipment from steam and minerals in the water can even determine how long the building lasts. The tubs at different concentrations are a consideration for guests, but behind the scenes, careful material choices are essential too.
