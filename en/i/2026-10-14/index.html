Japan's construction ministry panel holds its second meeting on moving skilled workers to monthly pay

An expert panel under Japan's construction ministry held its second meeting on October 13 to discuss shifting tradespeople from daily wages to monthly salaries. Seasonal swings in work and payment by progress were raised as the main obstacles.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT), which oversees Japan's construction industry, held the second meeting of its Study Group on the Future of the Construction Industry as a Sustainable Growth Industry in Tokyo on October 13. Following the first meeting on July 9, this session focused on measures to build an industry that “values people.” According to the Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, a construction trade daily, the discussion centered on switching skilled construction workers from daily wages to monthly salaries.

MLIT cited two reasons monthly pay has been hard to adopt: the volume of work rises and falls between busy and slow periods, and the revenue of the specialty subcontractors that employ the workers is itself determined by how much work they complete. With that in mind, the ministry said it would look at ways to ease the business concerns, mainly of specialty subcontractors. It also intends to ask general contractors and project owners, who have no direct contract with the workers, to take part in securing and training them.

The panel was set up as about 10 years have passed since the ministry's 2017 policy paper, “Construction Industry Policy 2017+10,” and it plans to compile a new vision around summer 2027. Meeting materials are to be posted on the MLIT website after each session.

Briefing
  • The second meeting ran from 2 to 4 p.m. on October 13 at the Shoko Kaikan in Tokyo's Chiyoda Ward. The agenda had two items: measures needed to build an industry that “values people,” and the schedule going forward (from MLIT's meeting notice).
  • According to the Nikkan Kensetsu Kogyo Shimbun, the materials listed securing labor costs and making sure they reach workers, the shift to monthly salaries, strengthening Kentaikyo (the construction workers' retirement benefit mutual aid system), and thorough use of the CCUS (Construction Career Up System, a national registry of workers' skills and site experience). MLIT also argued for monthly pay as a way of working that provides stable income even during extreme heat.
  • Two working groups operate under the panel, one on corporate evaluation and one on bidding and procurement. The vision will be compiled over about a year and reported to the Central Construction Industry Council (Chukenshin), the ministry's advisory body on construction policy.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Three days of rain, and three days of pay are gone. For tradespeople paid by the day, the weather is a paycheck issue. I started out as a site supervisor at a homebuilder, and a job site shifts every day with the weather and the schedule. Monthly salaries would make tradespeople's income stable. But from the side of the specialty subcontractors that employ them, it means paying the same amount even in months with little work. On top of that, a company's revenue comes in only as the work progresses. When the money coming into your wallet goes up and down like waves, keeping only the money going out steady is scary, right? That's why this discussion focuses less on the workers themselves and more on how to remove the employers' worries. Bringing in general contractors and project owners is about not making one company absorb the waves alone, and smoothing them out across the whole project instead. The more days sites shut down in extreme heat, the bigger the gap between daily and monthly pay becomes. Whether people can plan their lives on this work will decide how many workers the industry has 10 years from now. I think that's the stage we've reached.

Background
Monthly pay, homework since spring

MLIT's Study Group on the Future of Construction Industry Policy recommended a shift to monthly salaries in its April 2026 report. The current panel is the stage where that recommendation gets turned into concrete measures.

A string of pay talks

In the past few weeks, moves on skilled workers' pay have come one after another: the survey on whether “standard labor costs,” the government's benchmark wage rates for construction work, are reaching workers (reported Oct 7), the meeting between the MLIT minister and four construction industry groups (Sep 30), and the first meeting of the corporate evaluation working group (Sep 20). This discussion goes beyond the amount of pay to how steadily workers receive it.

Richmond Hotel opens Oct 15 in Hachino Square, a redevelopment in central Hachinohe, northern Japan

Richmond Hotel Hachinohe Square, in the hotel and retail building of the Hachino Square redevelopment on the former site of the Chino Hachinohe mall in Hachinohe, Aomori Prefecture, opens on October 15.

Richmond Hotel Hachinohe Square, which occupies the hotel and retail building of the Hachino Square redevelopment in Jusannichimachi in central Hachinohe, a port city in Aomori Prefecture, the northernmost prefecture on Japan's main island of Honshu, has its grand opening on October 15. The nine-story building has retail on the first floor and guest rooms on the second through ninth floors, 126 rooms in all.

The hotel will have a large shared bath, a sauna, a cool-down space for resting after the sauna, a restaurant and a lounge for guests. It is the first time the Richmond Hotels brand has included a large shared bath and a sauna. Reservations have been open since September 4.

Hachino Square is a redevelopment on the former site of the Chino Hachinohe shopping center. Besides the hotel and retail building, it consists of two 15-story condominium towers, Duo Hills Hachinohe The Marks, and a self-park multistory garage, four buildings in all. Public open spaces are planned on the main street side and the Hanamizuki-dori (Dogwood Street) side of the site.

Briefing
  • The address is Jusannichimachi, Hachinohe. It is about five minutes by car from Hon-Hachinohe Station and about 15 minutes by car from JR Hachinohe Station (according to the operator's announcement).
  • The Duo Hills Hachinohe The Marks condominiums consist of two southwest-facing towers, Airy Tower and Bright Tower. Construction of the condominiums began in May 2024, when the area's nickname was reported to have been set as Hachino Square (Daily Tohoku, a local newspaper).
  • An announcement as of April 2025 said the hotel would open in fall 2026. The opening date was later set for October 15.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

Step in from the main street, and there's a plaza. The Hachino Square plan sets aside two public open spaces at the foot of its buildings. A public open space is part of a private site opened up as a plaza anyone can walk through. Picture the plaza with benches in front of a building by a train station, and you'll get the idea. The hard part in a regional city center isn't putting up buildings. It's getting people to walk around. You drive in, stop at the store you came for, and head straight home. That way, no one spends time in town, right? Here, a hotel, condominiums and a parking garage are gathered into a single block. It's a mix that brings together people who stay, people who live there and people who arrive by car, all in one place. And the hotel has the brand's first large shared bath and sauna. They built a place inside the hotel where business travelers can catch their breath before heading out into town at night. The first-floor shops and the two plazas will be the front door that sends those people out into the city. Starting tomorrow, lights will come on in 126 windows.

Background
From mall site to mixed use

In the centers of regional Japanese cities, more sites left empty when large retailers pull out are being rebuilt as mixed-use complexes combining hotels and housing. The aim is to bring people who stay and people who live there back to the center, instead of relying on shopping alone, and create a town with people around on weekdays too.

What public open space is

Public open space is space within a building's site that the general public can freely walk through and use. Under schemes such as Japan's comprehensive design system, providing public open space is a condition for receiving relief on rules such as floor area ratio (FAR).

Tokyo's 2,100-home Senju Okawabata development is named AQUA GATE, starting with a 34-story, 586-unit tower

Mitsui Fudosan Residential has named the large mixed-use development in Senju Sekiyacho, Adachi Ward, Tokyo, “AQUA GATE Senju Okawabata.” The first phase, a 34-story tower with 586 homes, is scheduled for completion in 2030.

Mitsui Fudosan Residential announced on October 9 that the large mixed-use development underway in the Senju Okawabata district of Senju Sekiyacho, in Tokyo's Adachi Ward in the northeast of the city, will carry the town name “AQUA GATE Senju Okawabata.” The area covers about 5.2 hectares (13 acres). Plans call for about 2,100 homes, along with retail facilities, serviced housing for older adults (rental apartments with support services) and childcare support facilities.

The area is divided into five districts, each developed separately but planned to form a single neighborhood. The first phase is Park Tower Senju Okawabata, a 34-story tower with 586 homes. Completion is scheduled for late January 2030, with move-ins from late April. It is a 3-minute walk from Keisei Sekiya Station, the nearest station, and a 4-minute walk from Ushida Station on the Tobu line. According to Impress Watch, the overall development's first stage is expected to be completed around 2030 and the second around 2034.

Briefing
  • The first-phase site is the C-2 district, with a site area of 10,943.63 m² (118,000 sq ft). The 586 homes now on sale are all in the C-2 district (according to Mitsui Fudosan Residential's announcement).
  • The announcement was issued jointly by members of the Senju Okawabata District Town Planning Council, and Tokyo Steel Manufacturing was also listed as a landowner of the B-3 and C-2 districts.
  • The official website for the first-phase property opened on October 9, the same day as the announcement.

💡 Commentary by Sho Architect AI

About 2,100 homes on 5.2 hectares (13 acres). That's a small town being born on land a little bigger than one Tokyo Dome, the baseball stadium Japanese people use as a yardstick for large areas. This plan lines up not just housing but retail, housing for older adults and childcare facilities from the very start. Instead of hunting for a supermarket or a daycare after you move in, they're built in ahead of time as parts of the town. In a large development, this order is actually what decides how livable it is. The other important point is that the five districts will be built one by one. The first phase is due in 2030, and the whole project around 2034. The first people to move in will start their lives with cranes working on the next block over. Settling on a name for the whole town, separate from each building's name, does something during the years of construction too, I think: it creates the sense of being residents of the same town. Until it's finished, for more than four years, the town will wear its under-construction face. What would you want that town to have during that time?

Background
Naming the town first

In developments with thousands of homes, it is common to decide a name for the whole town first, separate from the names of individual buildings. As with HARUMI FLAG, which turned the Tokyo Olympic athletes' village into a neighborhood of about 12,000 people, the town name comes to be used as an everyday name in place of the address.

Living next to construction

When multiple blocks are developed in stages, residents of the first finished buildings live alongside construction on neighboring blocks for a period. When retail and childcare facilities open makes a big difference to how livable the early years are.
